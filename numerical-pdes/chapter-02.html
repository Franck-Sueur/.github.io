Chapter 2 Sobolev spaces

2.1 Introduction

Notation for derivatives.

Throughout the notes, partial derivatives are written with operator notation, for example ∂xu\partial_{x}u, ∂tu\partial_{t}u, ∂x⁢xu\partial_{xx}u, and Dα⁢uD^{\alpha}u. When the functions are smooth, these are classical derivatives. In Sobolev spaces, the same notation denotes weak derivatives. Subscript notation such as utu_{t} is not used for derivatives, since subscripts are also used for indexed families and homotopy parameters.

Let Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a bounded Lipschitz domain. In the finite element method, we do not usually work with classical solutions of boundary value problems. Instead, we rewrite the problem in weak form and look for a solution in a space where only a limited number of derivatives are required.

A model example is the Poisson problem

−Δ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω.-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. (2.1)

If uu and a test function vv are smooth and v=0v=0 on the boundary, integration by parts gives

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x.\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx. (2.2)

This identity only involves first derivatives of uu. It is therefore natural to look for uu in a space of square-integrable functions whose first derivatives are also square-integrable. This leads to the Sobolev space H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) and, when the boundary condition is homogeneous Dirichlet, to the subspace H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

2.2 Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces

2.2.1 Weak derivatives

Definition 2.1.

Let u∈Lloc1⁢(Ω)u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega) and let i∈{1,…,d}i\in\{1,\dots,d\}. A function g∈Lloc1⁢(Ω)g\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega) is called the weak partial derivative of uu with respect to xix_{i} if

∫Ωu⁢∂iφ⁢d⁢x=−∫Ωg⁢φ⁢𝑑xfor all ⁢φ∈Cc∞⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}u\,\partial_{i}\varphi\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}g\,\varphi\,dx\qquad% \text{for all }\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega). (2.3)

In that case we write g=∂iug=\partial_{i}u.

The definition is the same as saying that the distributional derivative of uu is represented by an Lloc1L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}} function.

Remark 2.2.

Weak derivatives are unique up to equality almost everywhere. Indeed, if g1g_{1} and g2g_{2} satisfy the defining identity, then

∫Ω(g1−g2)⁢φ⁢𝑑x=0for all ⁢φ∈Cc∞⁢(Ω),\int_{\Omega}(g_{1}-g_{2})\varphi\,dx=0\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in C_{c}^{% \infty}(\Omega), (2.4)

so g1=g2g_{1}=g_{2} almost everywhere.

Proposition 2.3.

Let u⁢(x)=|x|u(x)=\left|x\right| on (−1,1)(-1,1). Then uu is not differentiable at x=0x=0 in the classical sense, but it has the weak derivative

u′⁢(x)=sign⁡(x)a.e. on ⁢(−1,1).u^{\prime}(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)\qquad\text{a.e. on }(-1,1). (2.5)
Proof.

Let φ∈Cc∞⁢((−1,1))\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((-1,1)). Since u⁢(x)=−xu(x)=-x on (−1,0)(-1,0) and u⁢(x)=xu(x)=x on (0,1)(0,1), we have

∫−11|x|⁢φ′⁢(x)⁢𝑑x\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\left|x\right|\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,dx =∫−10(−x)⁢φ′⁢(x)⁢𝑑x+∫01x⁢φ′⁢(x)⁢𝑑x\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}(-x)\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}x\varphi^{% \prime}(x)\,dx (2.6)
=∫−10φ⁢(x)⁢𝑑x−∫01φ⁢(x)⁢𝑑x,\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{0}\varphi(x)\,dx-\int_{0}^{1}\varphi(x)\,dx, (2.7)

where the boundary terms vanish because φ\varphi has compact support in (−1,1)(-1,1). Therefore

∫−11|x|⁢φ′⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=−∫−11sign⁡(x)⁢φ⁢(x)⁢𝑑x,\int_{-1}^{1}\left|x\right|\,\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,dx=-\int_{-1}^{1}% \operatorname{sign}(x)\,\varphi(x)\,dx, (2.8)

which is exactly the defining identity. ∎

2.2.2 The spaces Wm,pW^{m,p} and HmH^{m}

We use the standard multi-index notation. If α=(α1,…,αd)∈ℕd\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}, then

|α|=α1+⋯+αd,Dα=∂1α1⋯⁢∂dαd.|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d},\qquad D^{\alpha}=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{% 1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}. (2.9)
Definition 2.4.

Let m∈ℕm\in\mathbb{N} and 1≤p≤∞1\leq p\leq\infty. The Sobolev space Wm,p⁢(Ω)W^{m,p}(\Omega) is defined by

Wm,p⁢(Ω)={u∈Lp⁢(Ω):Dα⁢u∈Lp⁢(Ω)⁢ for all ⁢α⁢ with ⁢|α|≤m},W^{m,p}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega):D^{\alpha}u\in L^{p}(\Omega)\text{ % for all }\alpha\text{ with }|\alpha|\leq m\right\}, (2.10)

where derivatives are understood in the weak sense. When p=2p=2, we write

Hm⁢(Ω)=Wm,2⁢(Ω).H^{m}(\Omega)=W^{m,2}(\Omega). (2.11)

For u∈Wm,p⁢(Ω)u\in W^{m,p}(\Omega), the norm is

‖u‖Wm,p⁢(Ω)={(∑|α|≤m‖Dα⁢u‖Lp⁢(Ω)p)1/p,1≤p<∞,max|α|≤m⁡‖Dα⁢u‖L∞⁢(Ω),p=∞.\left\|u\right\|_{W^{m,p}(\Omega)}=\begin{cases}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{|% \alpha|\leq m}\left\|D^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\right)^{1/p},&1% \leq p<\infty,\\ \displaystyle\max_{|\alpha|\leq m}\left\|D^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(% \Omega)},&p=\infty.\end{cases} (2.12)

In particular,

‖u‖Hm⁢(Ω)=(∑|α|≤m‖Dα⁢u‖L2⁢(Ω)2)1/2.\left\|u\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\left\|D^{\alpha}u% \right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}. (2.13)

The corresponding seminorm is

|u|Hm⁢(Ω)=(∑|α|=m‖Dα⁢u‖L2⁢(Ω)2)1/2.|u|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=m}\left\|D^{\alpha}u\right\|_{L^{2}(% \Omega)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}. (2.14)

For finite elements, the most important cases are H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) and H2⁢(Ω)H^{2}(\Omega). The first one is the natural space for weak formulations of second-order elliptic problems; the second one often appears in interpolation estimates for P1P_{1} elements.

Remark 2.5.

When Ω=I=(a,b)\Omega=I=(a,b) is an interval,

H1⁢(I)={u∈L2⁢(I):u′∈L2⁢(I)}H^{1}(I)=\{u\in L^{2}(I):u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(I)\} (2.15)

and

‖u‖H1⁢(I)2=‖u‖L2⁢(I)2+‖u′‖L2⁢(I)2.\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(I)}^{2}=\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}+\left\|u^{% \prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}. (2.16)

2.3 Hilbert structure and density

Theorem 2.6.

For every integer m≥0m\geq 0, the space Hm⁢(Ω)H^{m}(\Omega) is a Hilbert space for the scalar product

(u,v)Hm⁢(Ω)=∑|α|≤m∫ΩDα⁢u⁢Dα⁢v⁢𝑑x.(u,v)_{H^{m}(\Omega)}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}u\,D^{\alpha% }v\,dx. (2.17)
Proof.

Only completeness needs justification. Let (un)(u_{n}) be a Cauchy sequence in Hm⁢(Ω)H^{m}(\Omega). Then for every multi-index α\alpha with |α|≤m|\alpha|\leq m, the sequence (Dα⁢un)(D^{\alpha}u_{n}) is Cauchy in L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega). Since L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega) is complete, there exists gα∈L2⁢(Ω)g_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\Omega) such that

Dα⁢un→gαin ⁢L2⁢(Ω).D^{\alpha}u_{n}\to g_{\alpha}\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega). (2.18)

In particular, for α=0\alpha=0, we have un→g0u_{n}\to g_{0} in L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega). Set u=g0u=g_{0}.

We claim that Dα⁢u=gαD^{\alpha}u=g_{\alpha} in the weak sense. Let φ∈Cc∞⁢(Ω)\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega). Then

∫Ωu⁢Dα⁢φ⁢𝑑x\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\,D^{\alpha}\varphi\,dx =limn→∞∫Ωun⁢Dα⁢φ⁢𝑑x\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{n}\,D^{\alpha}\varphi\,dx (2.19)
=(−1)|α|⁢limn→∞∫ΩDα⁢un⁢φ⁢𝑑x\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}u_{n}\,% \varphi\,dx (2.20)
=(−1)|α|⁢∫Ωgα⁢φ⁢𝑑x.\displaystyle=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_{\Omega}g_{\alpha}\,\varphi\,dx. (2.21)

Thus gαg_{\alpha} is the weak derivative Dα⁢uD^{\alpha}u. Hence u∈Hm⁢(Ω)u\in H^{m}(\Omega). Finally,

‖un−u‖Hm⁢(Ω)2=∑|α|≤m‖Dα⁢un−gα‖L2⁢(Ω)2→0,\left\|u_{n}-u\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\left\|D^{% \alpha}u_{n}-g_{\alpha}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\to 0, (2.22)

so un→uu_{n}\to u in Hm⁢(Ω)H^{m}(\Omega). ∎

Definition 2.7.

The space H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) is the closure of Cc∞⁢(Ω)C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega) in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega):

H01⁢(Ω)=Cc∞⁢(Ω)¯H1⁢(Ω).H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)=\overline{C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,H^{1}(\Omega)}. (2.23)
Proposition 2.8.

The space H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) is a closed subspace of H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega), hence a Hilbert space.

Proof.

By definition, H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) is closed in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega). Since closed subspaces of Hilbert spaces are Hilbert, the conclusion follows. ∎

Theorem 2.9.

If Ω\Omega is a bounded Lipschitz domain, then C∞⁢(Ω¯)C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}) is dense in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega). Moreover, Cc∞⁢(Ω)C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega) is dense in H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) by definition.

2.4 The one-dimensional case: continuity and endpoint values

In one dimension, Sobolev spaces have a particularly concrete interpretation.

Theorem 2.10.

Let I=(a,b)I=(a,b) be a bounded interval and let u∈H1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}(I). Then uu admits a unique continuous representative on [a,b][a,b], still denoted by uu, such that

u⁢(x)=u⁢(y)+∫yxu′⁢(t)⁢𝑑tfor all ⁢x,y∈[a,b].u(x)=u(y)+\int_{y}^{x}u^{\prime}(t)\,dt\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in[a,b]. (2.24)

In particular, H1⁢(I)↪C0⁢([a,b])H^{1}(I)\hookrightarrow C^{0}([a,b]) continuously.

Remark 2.11.

In one dimension, the continuous representative of an H1H^{1} function is absolutely continuous. Its classical derivative exists almost everywhere and agrees almost everywhere with the weak derivative. Actually, the result above can be extended to the Sobolev space W1,1W^{1,1}.

Corollary 2.12.

There exists a constant CC, depending only on aa and bb, such that

‖u‖L∞⁢(I)≤C⁢‖u‖H1⁢(I)for all ⁢u∈H1⁢(I).\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leq C\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(I)}\qquad\text{% for all }u\in H^{1}(I). (2.25)

Thus the endpoint values u⁢(a)u(a) and u⁢(b)u(b) are well-defined and depend continuously on uu.

Theorem 2.13.

On a bounded interval I=(a,b)I=(a,b),

H01⁢(I)={u∈H1⁢(I):u⁢(a)=u⁢(b)=0}.H^{1}_{0}(I)=\{u\in H^{1}(I):u(a)=u(b)=0\}. (2.26)
Corollary 2.14.

If u∈H2⁢(I)u\in H^{2}(I), then u′∈H1⁢(I)u^{\prime}\in H^{1}(I) and therefore u′∈C0⁢([a,b])u^{\prime}\in C^{0}([a,b]). Hence

H2⁢(I)↪C1⁢([a,b]).H^{2}(I)\hookrightarrow C^{1}([a,b]). (2.27)

2.5 Trace theorem and boundary conditions in several dimensions

The one-dimensional picture extends to Lipschitz domains in a weaker but still very useful way.

Theorem 2.15.

Let Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a bounded Lipschitz domain. There exists a continuous linear map

γ:H1⁢(Ω)→L2⁢(∂Ω)\gamma:H^{1}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega) (2.28)

such that γ⁢u=u|∂Ω\gamma u=u|_{\partial\Omega} whenever uu is smooth up to the boundary. Moreover,

‖γ⁢u‖L2⁢(∂Ω)≤Ctr⁢‖u‖H1⁢(Ω)for all ⁢u∈H1⁢(Ω),\left\|\gamma u\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{\mathrm{tr}}\left\|u% \right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all }u\in H^{1}(\Omega), (2.29)

and

ker⁡γ=H01⁢(Ω).\ker\gamma=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). (2.30)

2.6 Poincaré and Poincaré–Wirtinger inequalities

2.6.1 The global inequalities

Theorem 2.16.

Let Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a bounded Lipschitz domain. There exists a constant CP>0C_{P}>0 such that

‖u‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢‖∇u‖L2⁢(Ω)for all ⁢u∈H01⁢(Ω).\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(% \Omega)}\qquad\text{for all }u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). (2.31)
Corollary 2.17.

The formula

(u,v)V:=∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x(u,v)_{V}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx (2.32)

defines a scalar product on V=H01⁢(Ω)V=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), and the associated norm is equivalent to the usual H1H^{1} norm.

Theorem 2.18.

Assume in addition that Ω\Omega is connected. There exists a constant CP⁢W>0C_{PW}>0 such that for every u∈H1⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}(\Omega),

‖u−uΩ‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢W⁢‖∇u‖L2⁢(Ω),uΩ:=1|Ω|⁢∫Ωu⁢𝑑x.\left\|u-u_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{PW}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_% {L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad u_{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u\,dx. (2.33)

This inequality says that on a connected domain, the gradient controls a function up to an additive constant.

2.6.2 Proofs in one dimension

Proposition 2.19.

Let I=(a,b)I=(a,b) and let u∈H01⁢(I)u\in H^{1}_{0}(I). Then

‖u‖L2⁢(I)≤(b−a)⁢‖u′‖L2⁢(I).\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq(b-a)\left\|u^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}. (2.34)
Proof.

Since u⁢(a)=0u(a)=0, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

u⁢(x)=∫axu′⁢(t)⁢𝑑t.u(x)=\int_{a}^{x}u^{\prime}(t)\,dt. (2.35)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|u⁢(x)|≤x−a⁢‖u′‖L2⁢(I)≤b−a⁢‖u′‖L2⁢(I).|u(x)|\leq\sqrt{x-a}\,\left\|u^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq\sqrt{b-a}\,% \left\|u^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}. (2.36)

We square and integrate in xx over II to obtain

‖u‖L2⁢(I)2≤(b−a)2⁢‖u′‖L2⁢(I)2.\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}\leq(b-a)^{2}\left\|u^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I% )}^{2}. (2.37)

∎

Proposition 2.20.

Let I=(a,b)I=(a,b) and let u∈H1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}(I). Denote by

uI=1b−a⁢∫abu⁢(x)⁢𝑑xu_{I}=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}u(x)\,dx (2.38)

the average of uu on II. Then

‖u−uI‖L2⁢(I)≤(b−a)⁢‖u′‖L2⁢(I).\left\|u-u_{I}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq(b-a)\left\|u^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}. (2.39)
Proof.

The continuous function u−uIu-u_{I} has zero average on II, so it must vanish at some point ξ∈[a,b]\xi\in[a,b]. Therefore,

u⁢(x)−uI=∫ξxu′⁢(t)⁢𝑑t.u(x)-u_{I}=\int_{\xi}^{x}u^{\prime}(t)\,dt. (2.40)

The same estimate as above then gives the result. ∎

2.6.3 Local forms used in interpolation estimates

For finite elements, we often need inequalities on a single mesh element.

Corollary 2.21.

Let KK be an interval, triangle, or tetrahedron of diameter hKh_{K}. Then

‖v−vK‖L2⁢(K)≤C⁢hK⁢|v|H1⁢(K)for all ⁢v∈H1⁢(K),\left\|v-v_{K}\right\|_{L^{2}(K)}\leq Ch_{K}|v|_{H^{1}(K)}\qquad\text{for all % }v\in H^{1}(K), (2.41)

where

vK=1|K|⁢∫Kv⁢𝑑x.v_{K}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}v\,dx. (2.42)

The constant CC depends only on the shape of a reference element; on an interval we may take C=1C=1.

Remark 2.22.

This local estimate is one of the basic tools in interpolation theory for finite elements. In one dimension it is exactly the estimate used to prove the H1H^{1} error bound for nodal interpolation.

2.6.4 The zero-mean space and Neumann problems

In this subsection, assume that Ω\Omega is connected. The pure Neumann Poisson problem is not coercive on all of H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega), because constants have zero gradient. The natural way to remove this indeterminacy is to work in the zero-mean subspace

H⋆1⁢(Ω)={u∈H1⁢(Ω):uΩ=0}.H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):u_{\Omega}=0\right\}. (2.43)

By the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality, the seminorm |u|H1⁢(Ω)=‖∇u‖L2⁢(Ω)|u|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)} is a norm on H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega).

Corollary 2.23.

The formula

(u,v)⋆:=∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x(u,v)_{\star}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx (2.44)

defines a scalar product on H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega), and the induced norm is equivalent to the full H1H^{1} norm.

Proof.

If (u,u)⋆=0(u,u)_{\star}=0, then ∇u=0\nabla u=0 almost everywhere, so uu is almost everywhere constant on the connected domain Ω\Omega. Since uΩ=0u_{\Omega}=0, this constant is zero. Thus (⋅,⋅)⋆(\cdot,\cdot)_{\star} is a scalar product. Norm equivalence follows from Poincaré–Wirtinger. ∎

For f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega), this is the functional framework for the weak formulation of the pure Neumann problem

−Δ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,∂nu=0on ⁢∂Ω.-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. (2.45)

For a smooth solution, integrating over Ω\Omega and using Green’s formula gives the compatibility condition

∫Ωf⁢𝑑x=0.\int_{\Omega}f\,dx=0. (2.46)

The weak formulation is then: find u∈H⋆1⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega) such that

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑xfor all ⁢v∈H⋆1⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx\qquad\text{for all % }v\in H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega). (2.47)

The right-hand side is continuous on H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega) because Poincaré–Wirtinger controls ‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)} by ‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

2.7 The dual space H−1⁢(Ω)H^{-1}(\Omega)

For many elliptic problems, the right-hand side does not need to belong to L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega). The natural dual space is

H−1⁢(Ω):=(H01⁢(Ω))′.H^{-1}(\Omega):=\bigl{(}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\bigr{)}^{\prime}. (2.48)

If F∈H−1⁢(Ω)F\in H^{-1}(\Omega) and v∈H01⁢(Ω)v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), the duality pairing is denoted by

⟨F,v⟩H−1,H01.\langle F,v\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}_{0}}. (2.49)

The corresponding norm is

‖F‖H−1⁢(Ω)=supv∈H01⁢(Ω),v≠0|⟨F,v⟩||v|H1⁢(Ω).\left\|F\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}=\sup_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\,v\neq 0}\frac% {|\langle F,v\rangle|}{|v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}. (2.50)
Proposition 2.24.

Every function f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega) defines an element of H−1⁢(Ω)H^{-1}(\Omega) through

⟨f,v⟩:=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x.\langle f,v\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx. (2.51)

Moreover,

‖f‖H−1⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω).\left\|f\right\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (2.52)
Proof.

For v∈H01⁢(Ω)v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), the Cauchy–Schwarz and Poincaré inequalities give

|∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x|≤‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢|v|H1⁢(Ω).\left|\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx\right|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|v% \right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}|v|_{H^{1}(% \Omega)}. (2.53)

This is exactly the required continuity estimate. ∎

Remark 2.25.

Thus the weak formulation of

−Δ⁢u=Fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u=F\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (2.54)

may be written for every F∈H−1⁢(Ω)F\in H^{-1}(\Omega) as

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x=⟨F,v⟩for all ⁢v∈H01⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\langle F,v\rangle\qquad\text{for all }% v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). (2.55)

The right-hand side no longer needs to be an integral against an L2L^{2} function.

2.8 Green formulas and weak formulations

The passage from strong to weak formulations relies on integration by parts in Sobolev spaces.

Theorem 2.26.

Let Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let u,v∈H1⁢(Ω)u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega). Then, for each i=1,…,di=1,\dots,d,

∫Ω∂iu⁢v⁢d⁢x=−∫Ωu⁢∂iv⁢d⁢x+∫∂Ωγ⁢(u)⁢γ⁢(v)⁢ni⁢𝑑s,\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}u\,v\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}u\,\partial_{i}v\,dx+\int_{% \partial\Omega}\gamma(u)\gamma(v)n_{i}\,ds, (2.56)

where n=(n1,…,nd)n=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d}) is the outward unit normal.

Remark 2.27.

The proof is by density: we first write the formula for smooth functions and then pass to the limit, using the continuity of the trace operator.

A more familiar form is obtained when uu is more regular.

Corollary 2.28.

If u∈H2⁢(Ω)u\in H^{2}(\Omega) and v∈H1⁢(Ω)v\in H^{1}(\Omega), then

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x=−∫Ω(Δ⁢u)⁢v⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ω∂nu⁢γ⁢(v)⁢d⁢s,\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)v\,dx+\int_{% \partial\Omega}\partial_{n}u\,\gamma(v)\,ds, (2.57)

where ∂nu=∇u⋅n\partial_{n}u=\nabla u\cdot n is the normal derivative.

Remark 2.29.

If v∈H01⁢(Ω)v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), then γ⁢(v)=0\gamma(v)=0, so the boundary term disappears. This is the key point in the weak formulation of homogeneous Dirichlet problems.

Consider, for instance,

−Δ⁢u+c⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u+cu=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (2.58)

where c∈L∞⁢(Ω)c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega) with c≥0c\geq 0 almost everywhere and f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega). The weak formulation reads: find u∈H01⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) such that

a⁢(u,v)=ℓ⁢(v)for all ⁢v∈H01⁢(Ω),a(u,v)=\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), (2.59)

with

a⁢(w,v)\displaystyle a(w,v) =∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢x+∫Ωc⁢w⁢v⁢𝑑x,\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\int_{\Omega}cwv\,dx, (2.60)
ℓ⁢(v)\displaystyle\ell(v) =∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x.\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx. (2.61)

By the Cauchy–Schwarz and Poincaré inequalities,

|a⁢(w,v)|\displaystyle|a(w,v)| ≤‖∇w‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)+‖c‖L∞⁢(Ω)⁢‖w‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla w\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla v\right% \|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2% }(\Omega)}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)} (2.62)
≤(1+‖c‖L∞⁢(Ω)⁢CP2)⁢|w|H1⁢(Ω)⁢|v|H1⁢(Ω),\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}C_{P}^{2}\right)% |w|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}|v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}, (2.63)

and

a⁢(v,v)=|v|H1⁢(Ω)2+∫Ωc⁢|v|2⁢𝑑x≥|v|H1⁢(Ω)2.a(v,v)=|v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}c|v|^{2}\,dx\geq|v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)% }^{2}. (2.64)

Thus the bilinear form is continuous and coercive on H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) for the energy norm

‖v‖V:=|v|H1⁢(Ω).\left\|v\right\|_{V}:=|v|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}. (2.65)

2.9 A few embeddings to keep in mind

Theorem 2.30.

Let I=(a,b)I=(a,b) be a bounded interval. Then

H1⁢(I)↪C0⁢([a,b]).H^{1}(I)\hookrightarrow C^{0}([a,b]). (2.66)

If moreover u∈H2⁢(I)u\in H^{2}(I), then

H2⁢(I)↪C1⁢([a,b]).H^{2}(I)\hookrightarrow C^{1}([a,b]). (2.67)
Theorem 2.31.

Let Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} be a bounded Lipschitz domain.

  1. (i)

    If d=2d=2 or 33, then H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) is not in general contained in C0⁢(Ω¯)C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}). Point values are therefore not defined for arbitrary H1H^{1} functions.

  2. (ii)

    If d≤3d\leq 3, then H2⁢(Ω)↪C0⁢(Ω¯)H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}).
