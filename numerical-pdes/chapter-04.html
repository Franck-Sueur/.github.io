4.1 The model problem

We fix an interval I=(a,b)I=(a,b) and we consider the boundary value problem

−u′′+c⁢u=fin ⁢I,u⁢(a)=u⁢(b)=0,-u^{\prime\prime}+cu=f\quad\text{in }I,\qquad u(a)=u(b)=0, (4.1)

where ff in L2⁢(I)L^{2}(I) and cc in L∞⁢(I)L^{\infty}(I) with c⁢(x)≥0c(x)\geq 0 almost everywhere.

Definition 4.1.

We set

V=H01⁢(I).V=H^{1}_{0}(I). (4.2)

On VV, we use the H01⁢(I)H^{1}_{0}(I) norm

‖v‖H01⁢(I):=‖v′‖L2⁢(I)=(∫ab|v′⁢(x)|2⁢𝑑x)1/2.\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}:=\left\|v^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}=\left(% \int_{a}^{b}|v^{\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}. (4.3)

On H01⁢(I)H^{1}_{0}(I), this is a norm by the Poincaré inequality.

We introduce the bilinear form and the linear form

a⁢(w,v)\displaystyle a(w,v) =∫abw′⁢(x)⁢v′⁢(x)⁢𝑑x+∫abc⁢(x)⁢w⁢(x)⁢v⁢(x)⁢𝑑x,\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}w^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx+\int_{a}^{b}c(x)w(x)v% (x)\,dx, (4.4)
ℓ⁢(v)\displaystyle\ell(v) =∫abf⁢(x)⁢v⁢(x)⁢𝑑x.\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx. (4.5)

The weak formulation is: find uu in VV such that

a⁢(u,v)=ℓ⁢(v)for all ⁢v∈V.a(u,v)=\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V. (4.6)
Theorem 4.2.

The weak formulation of Definition 4.1 admits a unique solution uu in VV. Moreover, uu belongs to H2⁢(I)H^{2}(I).

Proof.

Let CPC_{P} be a constant such that

‖v‖L2⁢(I)≤CP⁢‖v‖H01⁢(I)for all ⁢v∈V.\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq C_{P}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}\qquad% \text{for all }v\in V. (4.7)

For all w,vw,v in VV, we have

|a⁢(w,v)|\displaystyle|a(w,v)| ≤‖w′‖L2⁢(I)⁢‖v′‖L2⁢(I)+‖c‖L∞⁢(I)⁢‖w‖L2⁢(I)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(I)\displaystyle\leq\left\|w^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\left\|v^{\prime}\right\|% _{L^{2}(I)}+\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\left\|w\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\left\|% v\right\|_{L^{2}(I)} (4.8)
≤(1+‖c‖L∞⁢(I)⁢CP2)⁢‖w‖H01⁢(I)⁢‖v‖H01⁢(I).\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}C_{P}^{2}\right)\left% \|w\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}. (4.9)

Thus aa is continuous on V×VV\times V. Moreover,

a⁢(v,v)=‖v‖H01⁢(I)2+∫abc⁢(x)⁢|v⁢(x)|2⁢𝑑x≥‖v‖H01⁢(I)2,a(v,v)=\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}^{2}+\int_{a}^{b}c(x)|v(x)|^{2}\,dx\geq% \left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}^{2}, (4.10)

so aa is coercive on VV with coercivity constant α=1\alpha=1. Finally,

|ℓ⁢(v)|≤‖f‖L2⁢(I)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(I)≤CP⁢‖f‖L2⁢(I)⁢‖v‖H01⁢(I).|\ell(v)|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\left\|v\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq C_{P}% \left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\left\|v\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}. (4.11)

We conclude by the Lax–Milgram theorem.

Since uu solves the weak problem, we have −u′′=f−c⁢u-u^{\prime\prime}=f-cu in the sense of distributions. As f−c⁢uf-cu in L2⁢(I)L^{2}(I), it follows that u′′u^{\prime\prime} in L2⁢(I)L^{2}(I), hence uu in H2⁢(I)H^{2}(I). ∎

4.2 The mesh, the global finite element space, the hat basis, and the nodal interpolant

Definition 4.3.

A mesh of [a,b][a,b] is a partition

a=x0<x1<⋯<xN=b.a=x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}=b. (4.12)

For i=1,…,Ni=1,\dots,N, we set

Ki=(xi−1,xi),hi=xi−xi−1,h=max1≤i≤N⁡hi.K_{i}=(x_{i-1},x_{i}),\qquad h_{i}=x_{i}-x_{i-1},\qquad h=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}% h_{i}. (4.13)
Definition 4.4.

Let VV be the energy space of a weak formulation. A finite-dimensional space VhV_{h} is called conforming for this weak formulation if

Vh⊂V.V_{h}\subset V. (4.14)

The associated Galerkin method is then called conforming. Thus the discrete trial and test functions satisfy, by construction, all the conditions included in the space VV, in particular the essential boundary conditions. The continuous bilinear and linear forms can therefore be restricted directly to VhV_{h}.

Remark 4.5.

Conformity is relative to the weak formulation. For instance, in the standard homogeneous Dirichlet formulation of the one-dimensional model problem, the energy space is V=H01⁢(I)V=H^{1}_{0}(I), so a conforming space must be a subspace of H01⁢(I)H^{1}_{0}(I). For a mixed boundary value problem, the space VV would instead encode only the essential part of the boundary condition.

Definition 4.6.

The global conforming P1P_{1} finite element space is

Vh={vh∈C0⁢([a,b]):vh|Ki⁢ is affine for every ⁢i,vh⁢(a)=vh⁢(b)=0}.V_{h}=\left\{v_{h}\in C^{0}([a,b]):v_{h}|_{K_{i}}\text{ is affine for every }i% ,\ v_{h}(a)=v_{h}(b)=0\right\}. (4.15)

This space is conforming in the sense of Definition 4.4, because every continuous piecewise affine function belongs to H1⁢(I)H^{1}(I), and the endpoint conditions give Vh⊂H01⁢(I)=VV_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(I)=V.

Remark 4.7.

The constrained space VhV_{h} describes the admissible Galerkin trial and test functions. An implementation may nevertheless store values at every mesh node, including the endpoints. Its full nodal space is

Wh={wh∈C0⁢([a,b]):wh|Ki⁢ is affine for every ⁢i},dimWh=N+1,W_{h}=\{w_{h}\in C^{0}([a,b]):w_{h}|_{K_{i}}\text{ is affine for every }i\},% \qquad\dim W_{h}=N+1, (4.16)

and Vh={wh∈Wh:wh⁢(a)=wh⁢(b)=0}V_{h}=\{w_{h}\in W_{h}:w_{h}(a)=w_{h}(b)=0\} has dimension N−1N-1. Keeping endpoint entries in a vector does not make those entries free unknowns. Section 4.6.1 shows explicitly how full assembly followed by Dirichlet enforcement gives the same solution as the Galerkin method on VhV_{h}.

Definition 4.8.

For i=1,…,N−1i=1,\dots,N-1, the global hat function φi\varphi_{i} is the unique function in VhV_{h} such that

φi⁢(xj)=δi⁢jfor ⁢j=1,…,N−1.\varphi_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}\qquad\text{for }j=1,\dots,N-1. (4.17)

Equivalently,

φi⁢(x)={x−xi−1hi,x∈[xi−1,xi],xi+1−xhi+1,x∈[xi,xi+1],0,otherwise.\varphi_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x-x_{i-1}}{h_{i}},&x\in[x_{i-1},x_{i}],\\ \dfrac{x_{i+1}-x}{h_{i+1}},&x\in[x_{i},x_{i+1}],\\ 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} (4.18)

These are global basis functions attached to the interior nodes x1,…,xN−1x_{1},\dots,x_{N-1}.

Proposition 4.9.

The family (φi)i=1,…,N−1(\varphi_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N-1} is a basis of VhV_{h}. In particular,

dimVh=N−1.\dim V_{h}=N-1. (4.19)

Moreover, every function vhv_{h} in VhV_{h} satisfies the nodal representation formula

vh⁢(x)=∑i=1N−1vh⁢(xi)⁢φi⁢(x)for all ⁢x∈[a,b].v_{h}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}v_{h}(x_{i})\varphi_{i}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in[a% ,b]. (4.20)
Proof.

We first prove linear independence. Let (λi)i=1,…,N−1(\lambda_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N-1} be scalars such that

∑i=1N−1λi⁢φi=0.\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}=0. (4.21)

We evaluate at xjx_{j} with j=1,…,N−1j=1,\dots,N-1. Since φi⁢(xj)=δi⁢j\varphi_{i}(x_{j})=\delta_{ij}, we obtain

0=∑i=1N−1λi⁢φi⁢(xj)=∑i=1N−1λi⁢δi⁢j=λj.0=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{i}\varphi_{i}(x_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{i}% \delta_{ij}=\lambda_{j}. (4.22)

Hence all coefficients vanish.

We next prove that the family spans VhV_{h}. Let vhv_{h} in VhV_{h} and define

v~h:=∑i=1N−1vh⁢(xi)⁢φi.\widetilde{v}_{h}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}v_{h}(x_{i})\varphi_{i}. (4.23)

Then v~h\widetilde{v}_{h} in VhV_{h}. For each node xjx_{j}, with j=0,…,Nj=0,\dots,N, we have

Indeed this is clear for j=0j=0 and j=Nj=N, because both functions vanish there, and for 1≤j≤N−11\leq j\leq N-1 it follows from the Kronecker relation. The difference vh−v~hv_{h}-\widetilde{v}_{h} is affine on each KiK_{i} and vanishes at both endpoints of KiK_{i}, hence it vanishes identically on KiK_{i}. Therefore vh=v~hv_{h}=\widetilde{v}_{h} on [a,b][a,b]. ∎

Definition 4.10.

Because we work in one dimension, every function in H1⁢(I)H^{1}(I) has a continuous representative on [a,b][a,b]. We therefore define the nodal interpolation operator

Πh:H01⁢(I)→Vh\Pi_{h}:H^{1}_{0}(I)\to V_{h} (4.25)

(Πh⁢v)⁢(xi)=v⁢(xi)for ⁢i=0,…,N.(\Pi_{h}v)(x_{i})=v(x_{i})\qquad\text{for }i=0,\dots,N. (4.26)

Equivalently,

Πh⁢v=∑i=1N−1v⁢(xi)⁢φi.\Pi_{h}v=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}v(x_{i})\varphi_{i}. (4.27)
Definition 4.11.

The discrete Galerkin problem is: find uhu_{h} in VhV_{h} such that

‖u−uh‖H01⁢(I)≤C⁢infvh∈Vh‖u−vh‖H01⁢(I),\left\|u-u_{h}\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}\leq C\inf_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\left\|u-v_{h}% \right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(I)}, (4.34)

4.6 Reference element, local shape functions, local matrices, and assembly

The purpose of this section is to separate clearly the local finite element construction, which is performed on a single element, from the global basis introduced in Section 4.2. Local shape functions live on one element only. The global hat functions are obtained after assembly and are different objects.

P1⁢(K):={p:K→ℝ:p⁢(x)=α+β⁢x⁢ for some ⁢α,β∈ℝ}.P_{1}(K):=\left\{p:K\to\mathbb{R}:p(x)=\alpha+\beta x\text{ for some }\alpha,% \beta\in\mathbb{R}\right\}. (4.68)

Remark 4.24.

The functions ψ1(i)\psi_{1}^{(i)} and ψ2(i)\psi_{2}^{(i)} are defined only on Ki¯\overline{K_{i}}. They are local basis functions attached to the two local degrees of freedom of the element KiK_{i}. They are not global hat functions. A global hat function is obtained by gluing together the relevant local shape functions on the neighboring elements that share the same interior node.

Definition 4.25.

Since jj is in {1,…,N−1}\{1,\dots,N-1\}, the node xjx_{j} belongs to exactly two elements: KjK_{j}, where it is the right endpoint, and Kj+1K_{j+1}, where it is the left endpoint. This is the elementary index rule behind the assembly formulas below.

4.6.1 Full assembly and enforcement of Dirichlet conditions

The preceding formulas describe the reduced Galerkin matrix directly. For practical assembly, a useful route is to number all nodes first, assemble all local contributions, and then impose the prescribed nodal values. The two routes are algebraically equivalent. Constraints can also be incorporated while local contributions are assembled; full assembly followed by modification is a convenient teaching procedure, not a requirement imposed on every implementation.

4.7 The pure Neumann Poisson problem

We first isolate the genuinely Neumann case, because it is the case in which coercivity fails on the whole space unless constants are removed. In one dimension, the outward normal derivative is

∂nu⁢(a)=−u′⁢(a)and∂nu⁢(b)=u′⁢(b).\partial_{n}u(a)=-u^{\prime}(a)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\partial_{n}u(b)=u^{% \prime}(b). (4.149)

−u′′=fin ⁢I,∂nu⁢(a)=ga,∂nu⁢(b)=gb.-u^{\prime\prime}=f\quad\text{in }I,\qquad\partial_{n}u(a)=g_{a},\qquad% \partial_{n}u(b)=g_{b}. (4.150)

A necessary compatibility condition is obtained by testing the equation against the constant function 11, or equivalently by integrating the equation over II:

∫abf⁢(x)⁢𝑑x+ga+gb=0.\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx+g_{a}+g_{b}=0. (4.151)

Without this condition, no weak solution exists. Even when it holds, the solution is determined only up to an additive constant, since the derivative does not see constants.
