7.1 The scalar transport equation and the method of characteristics

We begin with the constant-coefficient transport problem

∂tu+a⁢∂xu=0in ⁢ℝ×(0,+∞),u⁢(x,0)=u0⁢(x),\partial_{t}u+a\,\partial_{x}u=0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}\times(0,+\infty),% \qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x), (7.1)

where aa in ℝ∖{0}\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} is constant.

Proposition 7.1.

Assume that u0u_{0} in C1⁢(ℝ)C^{1}(\mathbb{R}). Then the transport problem admits the unique C1C^{1} solution

u⁢(x,t)=u0⁢(x−a⁢t).u(x,t)=u_{0}(x-at). (7.2)
Proof.

Let x⁢(t)x(t) solve

d⁢xd⁢t=a,x⁢(0)=x0.\frac{dx}{dt}=a,\qquad x(0)=x_{0}. (7.3)

Then x⁢(t)=x0+a⁢tx(t)=x_{0}+at. Along such a curve,

dd⁢t⁢u⁢(x⁢(t),t)=∂tu⁢(x⁢(t),t)+a⁢∂xu⁢(x⁢(t),t)=0.\frac{d}{dt}u(x(t),t)=\partial_{t}u(x(t),t)+a\partial_{x}u(x(t),t)=0. (7.4)

Hence uu is constant along each characteristic line and

u⁢(x0+a⁢t,t)=u0⁢(x0).u(x_{0}+at,t)=u_{0}(x_{0}). (7.5)

Replacing x0x_{0} with x−a⁢tx-at gives the formula. Direct differentiation verifies the equation and the initial condition, and uniqueness follows from the same characteristic argument. ∎

The initial profile is translated at velocity aa without change of shape.

Remark 7.2.

On a bounded interval (α,β)(\alpha,\beta), the sign of aa determines where a boundary condition is imposed. If a>0a>0, the characteristics enter through x=αx=\alpha, so x=αx=\alpha is the inflow boundary and x=βx=\beta is the outflow boundary. If a<0a<0, the rôles are reversed. See [3, Chapter 13, Section 13.1] and [2, Chapter 10, Section 10.2].

7.2 An a priori estimate by the energy method

We next consider the transport-reaction problem

∂tu+a⁢∂xu+a0⁢u=fin ⁢(α,β)×(0,+∞),\partial_{t}u+a\,\partial_{x}u+a_{0}u=f\qquad\text{in }(\alpha,\beta)\times(0,% +\infty), (7.6)

with a>0a>0, a0≥0a_{0}\geq 0, initial datum

u⁢(x,0)=u0⁢(x)for ⁢x∈(α,β),u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\qquad\text{for }x\in(\alpha,\beta), (7.7)

and inflow boundary condition

u⁢(α,t)=ϕ⁢(t)for ⁢t>0.u(\alpha,t)=\phi(t)\qquad\text{for }t>0. (7.8)

Assume that uu is smooth enough. Then, for every t>0t>0,

(7.9)

Consequently, if f=0f=0 and ϕ=0\phi=0, then

‖u⁢(t)‖L2⁢(α,β)≤‖u0‖L2⁢(α,β)for all ⁢t≥0.\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(% \alpha,\beta)}\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0. (7.10)

If a0>0a_{0}>0, then

‖u⁢(t)‖L2⁢(α,β)2≤‖u0‖L2⁢(α,β)2+1a0⁢∫0t‖f⁢(s)‖L2⁢(α,β)2⁢𝑑s+a⁢∫0t|ϕ⁢(s)|2⁢𝑑s.\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(% \alpha,\beta)}^{2}+\frac{1}{a_{0}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|f(s)\right\|_{L^{2}(% \alpha,\beta)}^{2}ds+a\int_{0}^{t}|\phi(s)|^{2}ds. (7.11)
Proof.

We multiply the equation by uu and integrate over (α,β)(\alpha,\beta):

∫αβ∂tu⁢u⁢d⁢x+a⁢∫αβ∂xu⁢u⁢d⁢x+a0⁢∫αβu2⁢𝑑x=∫αβf⁢u⁢𝑑x.\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{t}u\,u\,dx+a\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{x}u% \,u\,dx+a_{0}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}u^{2}\,dx=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}fu\,dx. (7.12)

The first term is

∫αβ∂tu⁢u⁢d⁢x=12⁢dd⁢t⁢∫αβu2⁢𝑑x.\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{t}u\,u\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\alpha}% ^{\beta}u^{2}\,dx. (7.13)

The second term gives

Using u⁢(α,t)=ϕ⁢(t)u(\alpha,t)=\phi(t) yields the identity. If f=0f=0 and ϕ=0\phi=0, the right-hand side is nonpositive after dropping the outflow term, and the norm is nonincreasing. If a0>0a_{0}>0, Young’s inequality gives

∫αβf⁢u⁢𝑑x≤12⁢a0⁢‖f⁢(t)‖L2⁢(α,β)2+a02⁢‖u⁢(t)‖L2⁢(α,β)2,\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}fu\,dx\leq\frac{1}{2a_{0}}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(% \alpha,\beta)}^{2}+\frac{a_{0}}{2}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}, (7.15)

and integration in time gives the estimate. ∎

A corresponding estimate for variable coefficients is given in [3, Chapter 13, Section 13.1.1].

7.3 Linear hyperbolic systems and the wave equation

We consider a first-order linear system

∂tU+A⁢∂xU=0in ⁢ℝ×(0,+∞),U⁢(x,0)=U0⁢(x),\partial_{t}U+A\partial_{x}U=0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}\times(0,+\infty),% \qquad U(x,0)=U_{0}(x), (7.16)

where U⁢(x,t)U(x,t) in ℝp\mathbb{R}^{p} and AA in ℝp×p\mathbb{R}^{p\times p} is constant.

Definition 7.4.

The system is hyperbolic if the matrix AA is diagonalizable over ℝ\mathbb{R} and has only real eigenvalues.

Proposition 7.5.

Assume that A=T⁢Λ⁢T−1A=T\Lambda T^{-1}, where

Λ=diag⁡(λ1,…,λp),λk∈ℝ.\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}),\qquad\lambda_{k}% \in\mathbb{R}. (7.17)

If W=T−1⁢UW=T^{-1}U, then the components of WW satisfy

∂tWk+λk⁢∂xWk=0,k=1,…,p.\partial_{t}W_{k}+\lambda_{k}\partial_{x}W_{k}=0,\qquad k=1,\dots,p. (7.18)

In particular,

Wk⁢(x,t)=Wk⁢(x−λk⁢t,0),U⁢(x,t)=∑k=1pWk⁢(x−λk⁢t,0)⁢rk,W_{k}(x,t)=W_{k}(x-\lambda_{k}t,0),\qquad U(x,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}W_{k}(x-\lambda% _{k}t,0)r_{k}, (7.19)

where rkr_{k} is the kkth column of TT.

Proof.

Since U=T⁢WU=TW, we have

T⁢∂tW+A⁢T⁢∂xW=0.T\partial_{t}W+AT\partial_{x}W=0. (7.20)

Using A⁢T=T⁢ΛAT=T\Lambda and multiplying by T−1T^{-1} gives

∂tW+Λ⁢∂xW=0.\partial_{t}W+\Lambda\partial_{x}W=0. (7.21)

The diagonal structure gives one scalar transport equation for each component. ∎

On a bounded interval, the sign of each eigenvalue determines the boundary treatment: each positive eigenvalue requires one incoming condition at the left endpoint, and each negative eigenvalue requires one incoming condition at the right endpoint.

The wave equation as a hyperbolic system

The one-dimensional wave equation, with wave speed γ>0\gamma>0,

∂t⁢tu−γ2⁢∂x⁢xu=fin ⁢(α,β)×(0,+∞)\partial_{tt}u-\gamma^{2}\partial_{xx}u=f\qquad\text{in }(\alpha,\beta)\times(% 0,+\infty) (7.22)

is a second-order equation in the displacement uu. We set

v=∂tu,q=γ⁢∂xu,U=(vq).v=\partial_{t}u,\qquad q=\gamma\partial_{x}u,\qquad U=\begin{pmatrix}v\\ q\end{pmatrix}. (7.23)

Then

∂tU+(0−γ−γ0)⁢∂xU=(f0).\partial_{t}U+\begin{pmatrix}0&-\gamma\\ -\gamma&0\end{pmatrix}\partial_{x}U=\begin{pmatrix}f\\ 0\end{pmatrix}. (7.24)

In the homogeneous case f=0f=0, this is a first-order homogeneous hyperbolic system. The characteristic variables

z+=v−q,z−=v+qz_{+}=v-q,\qquad z_{-}=v+q (7.25)

satisfy, in the general forced case,

∂tz++γ⁢∂xz+=f,∂tz−−γ⁢∂xz−=f.\partial_{t}z_{+}+\gamma\partial_{x}z_{+}=f,\qquad\partial_{t}z_{-}-\gamma% \partial_{x}z_{-}=f. (7.26)

Thus z+z_{+} travels to the right and z−z_{-} travels to the left.

For a classical solution on (0,L)(0,L) with homogeneous Dirichlet data

u⁢(0,t)=u⁢(L,t)=0,u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0, (7.27)

we have

v⁢(0,t)=v⁢(L,t)=0.v(0,t)=v(L,t)=0. (7.28)

Since

v=z++z−2,v=\frac{z_{+}+z_{-}}{2}, (7.29)

the characteristic variables satisfy the reflection relations

z+⁢(0,t)=−z−⁢(0,t),z−⁢(L,t)=−z+⁢(L,t).z_{+}(0,t)=-z_{-}(0,t),\qquad z_{-}(L,t)=-z_{+}(L,t). (7.30)

Thus the incoming characteristic component is prescribed as the opposite of the outgoing component. For non-homogeneous data u⁢(0,t)=g0⁢(t)u(0,t)=g_{0}(t) and u⁢(L,t)=gL⁢(t)u(L,t)=g_{L}(t), the same computation gives

z+⁢(0,t)=2⁢g0′⁢(t)−z−⁢(0,t),z−⁢(L,t)=2⁢gL′⁢(t)−z+⁢(L,t).z_{+}(0,t)=2g_{0}^{\prime}(t)-z_{-}(0,t),\qquad z_{-}(L,t)=2g_{L}^{\prime}(t)-% z_{+}(L,t). (7.31)

For homogeneous Neumann data

∂xu⁢(0,t)=∂xu⁢(L,t)=0,\partial_{x}u(0,t)=\partial_{x}u(L,t)=0, (7.32)

we have q⁢(0,t)=q⁢(L,t)=0q(0,t)=q(L,t)=0. Since

q=z−−z+2,q=\frac{z_{-}-z_{+}}{2}, (7.33)

the characteristic variables satisfy

z+⁢(0,t)=z−⁢(0,t),z−⁢(L,t)=z+⁢(L,t).z_{+}(0,t)=z_{-}(0,t),\qquad z_{-}(L,t)=z_{+}(L,t). (7.34)

The Neumann condition is therefore also a reflection relation, but with the opposite sign from the Dirichlet case. For non-homogeneous data ∂xu⁢(0,t)=m0⁢(t)\partial_{x}u(0,t)=m_{0}(t) and ∂xu⁢(L,t)=mL⁢(t)\partial_{x}u(L,t)=m_{L}(t), we obtain

z+⁢(0,t)=z−⁢(0,t)−2⁢γ⁢m0⁢(t),z−⁢(L,t)=z+⁢(L,t)+2⁢γ⁢mL⁢(t).z_{+}(0,t)=z_{-}(0,t)-2\gamma m_{0}(t),\qquad z_{-}(L,t)=z_{+}(L,t)+2\gamma m_% {L}(t). (7.35)
Proposition 7.6.

Assume that uu is smooth enough. Then

dd⁢t⁢(12⁢∫αβ((∂tu)2+γ2⁢(∂xu)2)⁢𝑑x)=∫αβf⁢∂tu⁢d⁢x+γ2⁢[∂xu⁢∂tu]αβ.\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\left((\partial_{t}u)^{2}+% \gamma^{2}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\,dx\right)=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f\partial% _{t}u\,dx+\gamma^{2}[\partial_{x}u\partial_{t}u]_{\alpha}^{\beta}. (7.36)

In particular, if f=0f=0 and either u⁢(α,t)=u⁢(β,t)=0u(\alpha,t)=u(\beta,t)=0 or ∂xu⁢(α,t)=∂xu⁢(β,t)=0\partial_{x}u(\alpha,t)=\partial_{x}u(\beta,t)=0, then the wave energy

E⁢(t)=12⁢∫αβ((∂tu)2+γ2⁢(∂xu)2)⁢𝑑xE(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\left((\partial_{t}u)^{2}+\gamma^{2}(% \partial_{x}u)^{2}\right)\,dx (7.37)

is constant in time.

Proof.

We multiply the equation by ∂tu\partial_{t}u and integrate over (α,β)(\alpha,\beta):

∫αβ∂t⁢tu⁢∂tu⁢d⁢x−γ2⁢∫αβ∂x⁢xu⁢∂tu⁢d⁢x=∫αβf⁢∂tu⁢d⁢x.\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{tt}u\partial_{t}u\,dx-\gamma^{2}\int_{\alpha}^{% \beta}\partial_{xx}u\partial_{t}u\,dx=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}f\partial_{t}u\,dx. (7.38)

The first term is the derivative of 12⁢∫(∂tu)2\frac{1}{2}\int(\partial_{t}u)^{2}. Integration by parts gives

−γ2⁢∫αβ∂x⁢xu⁢∂tu⁢d⁢x=−γ2⁢[∂xu⁢∂tu]αβ+γ22⁢dd⁢t⁢∫αβ(∂xu)2⁢𝑑x.-\gamma^{2}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\partial_{xx}u\partial_{t}u\,dx=-\gamma^{2}[% \partial_{x}u\partial_{t}u]_{\alpha}^{\beta}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}% \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\partial_{x}u)^{2}\,dx. (7.39)

This gives the identity. For homogeneous Dirichlet data, ∂tu⁢(α,t)=∂tu⁢(β,t)=0\partial_{t}u(\alpha,t)=\partial_{t}u(\beta,t)=0. For homogeneous Neumann data, ∂xu⁢(α,t)=∂xu⁢(β,t)=0\partial_{x}u(\alpha,t)=\partial_{x}u(\beta,t)=0. In both cases, the boundary term vanishes. ∎

7.4 Uniform grids and classical finite difference schemes

We return to the scalar transport equation

∂tu+a⁢∂xu=0.\partial_{t}u+a\partial_{x}u=0. (7.40)

On the uniform grid

xj=j⁢h,tn=n⁢Δ⁢t,x_{j}=jh,\qquad t^{n}=n\Delta t, (7.41)

we denote the numerical approximation by UjnU_{j}^{n} and set

ν=a⁢Δ⁢th,λ=Δ⁢th.\nu=\frac{a\Delta t}{h},\qquad\lambda=\frac{\Delta t}{h}. (7.42)

The number ν=a⁢λ\nu=a\lambda is the Courant number. We compare the following four schemes in the same order throughout the chapter.

FTCS

The forward-time centered-space scheme is

Ujn+1−UjnΔ⁢t+a⁢Uj+1n−Uj−1n2⁢h=0,\frac{U_{j}^{n+1}-U_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+a\frac{U_{j+1}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}}{2h}=0, (7.43)

or equivalently

Ujn+1=Ujn−ν2⁢(Uj+1n−Uj−1n).U_{j}^{n+1}=U_{j}^{n}-\frac{\nu}{2}(U_{j+1}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}). (7.44)

The abbreviation FTCS is used to avoid confusion with finite elements.

Upwind

For a>0a>0, the upwind scheme is

Ujn+1−UjnΔ⁢t+a⁢Ujn−Uj−1nh=0,\frac{U_{j}^{n+1}-U_{j}^{n}}{\Delta t}+a\frac{U_{j}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}}{h}=0, (7.45)

or

Ujn+1=Ujn−ν⁢(Ujn−Uj−1n).U_{j}^{n+1}=U_{j}^{n}-\nu(U_{j}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}). (7.46)

If a<0a<0, the upwind difference is the forward difference. The subscript UU in quantities such as gUg_{U} stands for upwind.

Lax–Friedrichs

The Lax–Friedrichs scheme is

Ujn+1−12⁢(Uj+1n+Uj−1n)Δ⁢t+a⁢Uj+1n−Uj−1n2⁢h=0,\frac{U_{j}^{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}(U_{j+1}^{n}+U_{j-1}^{n})}{\Delta t}+a\frac{U_{j+% 1}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}}{2h}=0, (7.47)

or

Ujn+1=12⁢(Uj+1n+Uj−1n)−ν2⁢(Uj+1n−Uj−1n).U_{j}^{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}(U_{j+1}^{n}+U_{j-1}^{n})-\frac{\nu}{2}(U_{j+1}^{n}-U_{% j-1}^{n}). (7.48)
Lax–Wendroff

The Lax–Wendroff scheme is

Ujn+1=Ujn−ν2⁢(Uj+1n−Uj−1n)+ν22⁢(Uj+1n−2⁢Ujn+Uj−1n).U_{j}^{n+1}=U_{j}^{n}-\frac{\nu}{2}(U_{j+1}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n})+\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}% (U_{j+1}^{n}-2U_{j}^{n}+U_{j-1}^{n}). (7.49)

It is obtained by a second-order Taylor expansion in time, together with ∂tu=−a⁢∂xu\partial_{t}u=-a\partial_{x}u and ∂t⁢tu=a2⁢∂x⁢xu\partial_{tt}u=a^{2}\partial_{xx}u.

On a finite interval with a>0a>0, the inflow value is prescribed at the left boundary and no condition is imposed at the outflow boundary. The upwind scheme simply uses the prescribed left value. Centered three-point schemes such as FTCS, Lax–Friedrichs, and Lax–Wendroff require an adapted boundary closure or a ghost-cell construction near the boundary. For systems, discrete boundary conditions are imposed on incoming characteristic components only. See [3, Chapter 13, Section 13.3.3]. Section 7.7.1 below works out and analyses two concrete outflow closures.

7.5 Consistency and equivalent equations

Definition 7.7.

An interior single-step finite difference scheme on the uniform grid is specified by a finite stencil 𝒜⊂ℤ\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z} and by an update map

ℋh,Δ⁢t,jn:ℝ𝒜→ℝ.\mathcal{H}_{h,\Delta t,j}^{n}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\to\mathbb{R}. (7.50)

The numerical values satisfy

Ujn+1=ℋh,Δ⁢t,jn⁢((Uj+rn)r∈𝒜).U_{j}^{n+1}=\mathcal{H}_{h,\Delta t,j}^{n}\left((U_{j+r}^{n})_{r\in\mathcal{A}% }\right). (7.51)

For an autonomous constant-coefficient scheme, this map does not depend on jj or nn.

For the four schemes introduced above, the update maps are

For a>0a>0,

Definition 7.8.

Let ℋh,Δ⁢t,jn\mathcal{H}_{h,\Delta t,j}^{n} be the update map of an interior single-step scheme. For a two-time-level grid function W=(Wjn)W=(W_{j}^{n}), the normalized residual is

Resh,Δ⁢t,jn⁡(U)=0.\operatorname{Res}_{h,\Delta t,j}^{n}(U)=0. (7.57)
Definition 7.9.

Let uu be a smooth exact solution of the differential equation and let

(Ih⁢u)jn=u⁢(xj,tn)(I_{h}u)_{j}^{n}=u(x_{j},t^{n}) (7.58)

be its grid restriction. The local truncation error is the normalized residual obtained after inserting the exact grid values into the numerical update:

τjn⁢(u)=Resh,Δ⁢t,jn⁡(Ih⁢u).\tau_{j}^{n}(u)=\operatorname{Res}_{h,\Delta t,j}^{n}(I_{h}u). (7.59)

Equivalently,

7.6 The CFL condition and numerical domains of dependence

The Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition expresses that the numerical domain of dependence must contain the exact domain of dependence in the limit. Here we formulate the usual necessity statement under the hyperbolic scaling Δ⁢t=λ⁢h\Delta t=\lambda h with fixed λ>0\lambda>0, hence fixed Courant number ν=a⁢λ\nu=a\lambda. For a stencil contained in {j−1,j,j+1}\{j-1,j,j+1\}, the numerical value after nn steps depends only on initial nodes in [xj−n⁢h,xj+n⁢h][x_{j}-nh,x_{j}+nh]. The exact value depends on the initial point xj−a⁢n⁢Δ⁢tx_{j}-an\Delta t. Consequently, convergence for every initial datum requires

|a|⁢Δ⁢t≤h,that is|ν|≤1.|a|\Delta t\leq h,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad|\nu|\leq 1. (7.82)

Indeed, if |ν|>1|\nu|>1 is fixed, at a positive fixed time the exact characteristic foot lies outside the numerical domain by a positive distance. Initial data supported there are invisible to the scheme but affect the exact solution. This argument can be made on the whole line, or on a periodic interval for times small enough that the relevant domains do not wrap around the interval.

Under this fixed-Courant scaling, CFL is necessary for convergence for all data, but is not sufficient by itself. FTCS satisfies the same geometric restriction, yet the von Neumann analysis below shows that it is not stable in ℓh2\ell_{h}^{2} for fixed nonzero ν\nu.

Remark 7.15 (Courant numbers depending on the mesh).

An exact inequality |νh|≤1|\nu_{h}|\leq 1 on every mesh is not necessary for finite-time convergence when νh\nu_{h} varies with hh. For example, on the unit periodic interval with a>0a>0, take upwind with νh=1+h\nu_{h}=1+h and Δ⁢th=h⁢(1+h)/a\Delta t_{h}=h(1+h)/a. The one-step operator satisfies

‖Gh‖ℓh2→ℓh2≤|1−νh|+νh=1+2⁢h,‖Ghn‖≤(1+2⁢h)n≤e2⁢a⁢T(n⁢Δ⁢th≤T).\|G_{h}\|_{\ell_{h}^{2}\to\ell_{h}^{2}}\leq|1-\nu_{h}|+\nu_{h}=1+2h,\qquad\|G_% {h}^{n}\|\leq(1+2h)^{n}\leq e^{2aT}\quad(n\Delta t_{h}\leq T). (7.83)

The scheme is stable on finite time intervals and its normalized residual for smooth transport solutions is O⁢(h)O(h). It therefore converges despite the small CFL violation on every grid. Its numerical domain approaches the exact one as h→0h\to 0, since νh→1\nu_{h}\to 1. This does not contradict the fixed-Courant necessity statement above.
