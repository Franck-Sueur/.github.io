Chapter 3 Lax–Milgram in action for a few elliptic problems

This chapter applies the Lax–Milgram theorem proved in Chapter 1 to a few standard elliptic boundary value problems. The functional framework of Chapter 2 is used repeatedly: the Poincaré inequality on H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality on H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega), and the trace theorem on bounded Lipschitz domains.

3.1 Homogeneous Dirichlet problems

We work on a bounded Lipschitz domain Ω⊂ℝd\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

3.1.1 The pure Poisson equation

We consider

−Δ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (3.1)

with f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega). The weak formulation is posed on

V=H01⁢(Ω),V=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), (3.2)

with

a⁢(w,v)\displaystyle a(w,v) =∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢x,\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx, (3.3)
ℓ⁢(v)\displaystyle\ell(v) =∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x.\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx. (3.4)
Proposition 3.1.

The weak formulation

a⁢(u,v)=ℓ⁢(v)for all ⁢v∈Va(u,v)=\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V (3.5)

admits a unique solution u∈H01⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Proof.

The bilinear form is continuous by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

|a⁢(w,v)|≤‖∇w‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω).|a(w,v)|\leq\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.6)

On H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), the Poincaré inequality of Chapter 2 shows that ∥∇⋅∥L2⁢(Ω)\|\nabla\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)} is a norm equivalent to the full H1H^{1} norm. Hence

a⁢(v,v)=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2≥α⁢‖v‖H1⁢(Ω)2a(v,v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\geq\alpha\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2} (3.7)

for some α>0\alpha>0. Finally,

|ℓ⁢(v)|≤‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω).|\ell(v)|\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\|f\|_{L^{2}(% \Omega)}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.8)

We conclude by Theorem 1.2. ∎

Remark 3.2.

This is the basic Dirichlet model. The coercivity does not come from the differential operator alone; it comes from the choice of the space H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

3.1.2 Adding a nonnegative lower-order term

We now consider

−Δ⁢u+c⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u+cu=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (3.9)

where c∈L∞⁢(Ω)c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega) and c⁢(x)≥0c(x)\geq 0 almost everywhere. The weak formulation is again posed on V=H01⁢(Ω)V=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega), with

a⁢(w,v)=∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢x+∫Ωc⁢w⁢v⁢𝑑x.a(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\int_{\Omega}cwv\,dx. (3.10)
Proposition 3.3.

The associated weak problem has a unique solution in H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Proof.

Continuity is immediate:

|a⁢(w,v)|≤‖∇w‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)+‖c‖L∞⁢(Ω)⁢‖w‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω).|a(w,v)|\leq\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|c\|_{L^% {\infty}(\Omega)}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.11)

The Poincaré inequality absorbs the L2L^{2} terms. For coercivity we simply write

a⁢(v,v)=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2+∫Ωc⁢|v|2⁢𝑑x≥‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2.a(v,v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\int_{\Omega}c|v|^{2}\,dx\geq\|\nabla v% \|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}. (3.12)

Thus Theorem 1.2 applies exactly as before. ∎

Remark 3.4.

The case c≡1c\equiv 1 is especially useful pedagogically. The lower-order term does not change the Dirichlet space, but it completely changes the Neumann and periodic situations.

3.1.3 A negative lower-order term

The sign of the lower-order term matters. Consider

−Δ⁢u−λ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u-\lambda u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (3.13)

with λ≥0\lambda\geq 0. The natural bilinear form is

aλ⁢(w,v)=∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢x−λ⁢∫Ωw⁢v⁢𝑑x.a_{\lambda}(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx-\lambda\int_{\Omega}wv% \,dx. (3.14)
Proposition 3.5.

Assume that λ<CP−2\lambda<C_{P}^{-2}, where CPC_{P} is a Poincaré constant on H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega):

‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)for all ⁢v∈H01⁢(Ω).\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all% }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). (3.15)

Then aλa_{\lambda} is coercive on H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). In particular, the weak problem has a unique solution.

Proof.

For every v∈H01⁢(Ω)v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

aλ⁢(v,v)=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2−λ⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)2≥(1−λ⁢CP2)⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2.a_{\lambda}(v,v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\lambda\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}% ^{2}\geq\bigl{(}1-\lambda C_{P}^{2}\bigr{)}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}. (3.16)

The factor in parentheses is positive by assumption, so coercivity follows. ∎

Remark 3.6.

This is the simplest place where the Lax–Milgram framework may fail. If λ\lambda is too large, the form is no longer coercive. With the optimal Poincaré constant, the threshold is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue λ1⁢(Ω)\lambda_{1}(\Omega). On an interval I=(a,b)I=(a,b), we have λ1⁢(I)=π2/(b−a)2\lambda_{1}(I)=\pi^{2}/(b-a)^{2}.

3.2 Pure Neumann problems

In this section we assume in addition that Ω\Omega is connected.

3.2.1 The problem with a coercive lower-order term

We first treat

−Δ⁢u+u=fin ⁢Ω,∂nu=gon ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u+u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}u=g\quad\text{on }% \partial\Omega, (3.17)

where f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega) and g∈L2⁢(∂Ω)g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega). The weak formulation is posed on the full space

V=H1⁢(Ω),V=H^{1}(\Omega), (3.18)

with

a⁢(w,v)\displaystyle a(w,v) =∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢x+∫Ωw⁢v⁢𝑑x,\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\int_{\Omega}wv\,dx, (3.19)
ℓ⁢(v)\displaystyle\ell(v) =∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ωg⁢v⁢𝑑s.\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}gv\,ds. (3.20)
Proposition 3.7.

The weak problem has a unique solution u∈H1⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}(\Omega).

Proof.

The bilinear form is continuous on H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega). For the linear form, the trace theorem of Chapter 2 gives

‖v‖L2⁢(∂Ω)≤Ctr⁢‖v‖H1⁢(Ω),\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C_{\operatorname{tr}}\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}, (3.21)

so

|ℓ⁢(v)|≤‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)+‖g‖L2⁢(∂Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(∂Ω)≤C⁢‖v‖H1⁢(Ω).|\ell(v)|\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial% \Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}. (3.22)

Finally,

a⁢(v,v)=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2+‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)2=‖v‖H1⁢(Ω)2,a(v,v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\|v\|_{H^{1}% (\Omega)}^{2}, (3.23)

so aa is coercive on the whole space H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega). Theorem 1.2 therefore applies directly. ∎

Remark 3.8.

This example is worth isolating. The Neumann boundary condition does not create any difficulty here because the term +u+u removes the constant kernel.

3.2.2 The pure Neumann Poisson equation

We now remove the lower-order term and consider

−Δ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,∂nu=gon ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad\partial_{n}u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (3.24)

with f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega) and g∈L2⁢(∂Ω)g\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega). The formal weak formulation is

∫Ω∇u⋅∇φ⁢d⁢x=∫Ωf⁢φ⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ωg⁢φ⁢𝑑sfor all ⁢φ∈H1⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}f\varphi\,dx+\int_{% \partial\Omega}g\varphi\,ds\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega). (3.25)

However, on H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) the form

a⁢(w,v)=∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢xa(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx (3.26)

is not coercive, because every constant belongs to its kernel.

We first read the compatibility condition in the original weak formulation, where the constant function 11 is an admissible test function. If uu is a weak solution in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega), then taking φ=1\varphi=1 gives

∫Ωf⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ωg⁢𝑑s=0.\int_{\Omega}f\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,ds=0. (3.27)

To recover coercivity we introduce the mean-zero space

H⋆1⁢(Ω)={v∈H1⁢(Ω):∫Ωv⁢𝑑x=0}.H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega)=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\int_{\Omega}v\,dx=0\right\}. (3.28)

On this space, the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality of Chapter 2 yields

‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CP⁢W⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω).\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{PW}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.29)
Theorem 3.9.

The pure Neumann problem admits a weak solution if and only if

∫Ωf⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ωg⁢𝑑s=0.\int_{\Omega}f\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}g\,ds=0. (3.30)

When this condition holds, there exists a unique solution u∈H⋆1⁢(Ω)u\in H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega), and every weak solution in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) differs from uu by an additive constant.

Proof.

Necessity of the compatibility condition was established by testing with φ=1\varphi=1.

Assume now that the compatibility condition holds. We work on V=H⋆1⁢(Ω)V=H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega) with the norm

‖v‖V:=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω).\|v\|_{V}:=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.31)

The bilinear form aa is continuous on VV, and

a⁢(v,v)=‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)2=‖v‖V2for all ⁢v∈V,a(v,v)=\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\|v\|_{V}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }v\in V, (3.32)

so aa is coercive on VV. For the linear form

ℓ⁢(v)=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x+∫∂Ωg⁢v⁢𝑑s,\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}gv\,ds, (3.33)

the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality and the trace theorem give

|ℓ⁢(v)|≤‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)+‖g‖L2⁢(∂Ω)⁢‖v‖L2⁢(∂Ω)≤C⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)=C⁢‖v‖V.|\ell(v)|\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial% \Omega)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C\|v\|% _{V}. (3.34)

Hence Theorem 1.2 yields a unique u∈Vu\in V such that

a⁢(u,v)=ℓ⁢(v)for all ⁢v∈H⋆1⁢(Ω).a(u,v)=\ell(v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega). (3.35)

It remains to check that this solution satisfies the original weak formulation on the whole space H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega). Let φ∈H1⁢(Ω)\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega) and define the average

φ¯:=1|Ω|⁢∫Ωφ⁢𝑑x.\overline{\varphi}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dx. (3.36)

Then φ−φ¯∈H⋆1⁢(Ω)\varphi-\overline{\varphi}\in H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega) and

a⁢(u,φ)\displaystyle a(u,\varphi) =a⁢(u,φ−φ¯)=ℓ⁢(φ−φ¯)=ℓ⁢(φ)−φ¯⁢ℓ⁢(1).\displaystyle=a(u,\varphi-\overline{\varphi})=\ell(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})% =\ell(\varphi)-\overline{\varphi}\,\ell(1). (3.37)

Since the compatibility condition is exactly ℓ⁢(1)=0\ell(1)=0, we obtain

a⁢(u,φ)=ℓ⁢(φ)for all ⁢φ∈H1⁢(Ω).a(u,\varphi)=\ell(\varphi)\qquad\text{for all }\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega). (3.38)

Finally, if u1u_{1} and u2u_{2} are two weak solutions in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega), then

∫Ω∇(u1−u2)⋅∇φ⁢d⁢x=0for all ⁢φ∈H1⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=0\qquad\text{for all }% \varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega). (3.39)

Taking φ=u1−u2\varphi=u_{1}-u_{2} gives ∇(u1−u2)=0\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})=0 almost everywhere. Since Ω\Omega is connected, u1−u2u_{1}-u_{2} is constant. ∎

Remark 3.10.

The compatibility condition is not needed to solve the reduced problem on H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega). It is needed exactly when we pass from test functions in H⋆1⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{\star}(\Omega) to arbitrary test functions in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) through the decomposition

φ=(φ−φ¯)+φ¯.\varphi=(\varphi-\overline{\varphi})+\overline{\varphi}.
Remark 3.11.

In one dimension, on I=(a,b)I=(a,b), the homogeneous Neumann problem

−u′′+u=f,u′⁢(a)=u′⁢(b)=0,-u^{\prime\prime}+u=f,\qquad u^{\prime}(a)=u^{\prime}(b)=0,

is treated in H1⁢(I)H^{1}(I) in the same way. Without the term +u+u, constants again form the kernel and the zero-mean normalization becomes necessary.

3.3 Mixed Dirichlet–Neumann problems

In this section, assume that Ω\Omega is a bounded connected Lipschitz domain. Let the boundary be decomposed as

∂Ω=ΓD∪ΓN∪Σ,ΓD∩ΓN=∅,\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}\cup\Sigma,\qquad\Gamma_{D}\cap\Gamma_{% N}=\varnothing, (3.40)

where ΓD\Gamma_{D} and ΓN\Gamma_{N} are relatively open, the interface Σ=∂Ω∖(ΓD∪ΓN)\Sigma=\partial\Omega\setminus(\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}) has zero surface measure, and ΓD\Gamma_{D} has positive surface measure. This includes the usual mixed boundary conditions on adjacent boundary segments: their meeting points belong to Σ\Sigma. We consider

−Δ⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢ΓD,∂nu=gon ⁢ΓN,-\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{D},\qquad% \partial_{n}u=g\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{N}, (3.41)

with f∈L2⁢(Ω)f\in L^{2}(\Omega) and g∈L2⁢(ΓN)g\in L^{2}(\Gamma_{N}).

The natural space is

VΓD={v∈H1⁢(Ω):γ⁢v=0⁢ on ⁢ΓD}.V_{\Gamma_{D}}=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega):\gamma v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{D}\}. (3.42)

The assumption that ΓD\Gamma_{D} has positive surface measure is used through the following Poincaré inequality with partial homogeneous trace: there exists a constant CΓD>0C_{\Gamma_{D}}>0 such that

‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CΓD⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)for all ⁢v∈VΓD.\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Gamma_{D}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad% \text{for all }v\in V_{\Gamma_{D}}. (3.43)

Consequently ‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)} is a norm on VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}, equivalent to the usual H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega) norm. The positivity of the measure of ΓD\Gamma_{D} is essential. If the Dirichlet part is empty, or if it is invisible for the trace in the sense of surface measure, nonzero constants have zero gradient and are not excluded by the boundary condition, so (3.43) cannot hold.

A standard proof of (3.43) is by contradiction. If the inequality failed, there would exist vnv_{n} in VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}} such that ‖vn‖L2⁢(Ω)=1\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1 and ‖∇vn‖L2⁢(Ω)→0\|\nabla v_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0. The sequence is bounded in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega); by the Rellich compactness theorem, up to a subsequence, it converges strongly in L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega) to some vv. Passing to the limit in weak derivatives gives ∇v=0\nabla v=0. Since Ω\Omega is connected, vv is constant. In fact vn→vv_{n}\to v in H1⁢(Ω)H^{1}(\Omega), since both the functions and their gradients converge in L2L^{2}. The continuity of the trace operator therefore gives γ⁢v=0\gamma v=0 on ΓD\Gamma_{D}. Because ΓD\Gamma_{D} has positive surface measure, this constant must be zero, contradicting ‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)=1\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1. References for this form of the Poincaré inequality include [5, Chapter 9] and [2, Chapter 3].

The weak formulation is

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x+∫ΓNg⁢v⁢𝑑sfor all ⁢v∈VΓD.\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx+\int_{\Gamma_{N}}gv% \,ds\qquad\text{for all }v\in V_{\Gamma_{D}}. (3.44)
Proposition 3.12.

Assume that ΓD\Gamma_{D} has positive measure. Then the mixed weak problem has a unique solution u∈VΓDu\in V_{\Gamma_{D}}.

Proof.

We use the Poincaré inequality on VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}: there exists CΓD>0C_{\Gamma_{D}}>0 such that

‖v‖L2⁢(Ω)≤CΓD⁢‖∇v‖L2⁢(Ω)for all ⁢v∈VΓD.\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Gamma_{D}}\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad% \text{for all }v\in V_{\Gamma_{D}}. (3.45)

Thus the bilinear form

a⁢(w,v)=∫Ω∇w⋅∇v⁢d⁢xa(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla v\,dx (3.46)

is continuous and coercive on VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}. The linear form

ℓ⁢(v)=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑x+∫ΓNg⁢v⁢𝑑s\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx+\int_{\Gamma_{N}}gv\,ds (3.47)

is continuous by the trace theorem on ΓN\Gamma_{N} together with the Poincaré inequality on VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}. Theorem 1.2 then yields existence and uniqueness. ∎

Remark 3.13.

This is the basic model for the distinction between essential and natural boundary conditions. The Dirichlet part is built into the space VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}, while the Neumann part appears in the right-hand side after integration by parts.

Remark 3.14.

If nonzero Dirichlet data are prescribed on ΓD\Gamma_{D}, we first choose a lifting GG with the correct trace on ΓD\Gamma_{D} and then solve for U=u−GU=u-G in the homogeneous space VΓDV_{\Gamma_{D}}.

3.4 Periodic problems

We treat the periodic case in one dimension, where the functional setting is especially transparent. Let I=(0,1)I=(0,1) and define

Hper1⁢(I)={v∈H1⁢(I):v⁢(0)=v⁢(1)}.H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I)=\{v\in H^{1}(I):v(0)=v(1)\}. (3.48)

3.4.1 Periodic reaction–diffusion

Consider

−u′′+u=fin ⁢I,u⁢(0)=u⁢(1),u′⁢(0)=u′⁢(1),-u^{\prime\prime}+u=f\quad\text{in }I,\qquad u(0)=u(1),\qquad u^{\prime}(0)=u^% {\prime}(1), (3.49)

with f∈L2⁢(I)f\in L^{2}(I). The weak formulation is: find u∈Hper1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I) such that

∫01u′⁢v′⁢𝑑x+∫01u⁢v⁢𝑑x=∫01f⁢v⁢𝑑xfor all ⁢v∈Hper1⁢(I).\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}uv\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}fv\,dx% \qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I). (3.50)
Proposition 3.15.

The periodic reaction–diffusion problem has a unique weak solution in Hper1⁢(I)H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I).

Proof.

On Hper1⁢(I)H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I) we use the full H1H^{1} norm. The bilinear form is continuous and satisfies

a⁢(v,v)=‖v′‖L2⁢(I)2+‖v‖L2⁢(I)2=‖v‖H1⁢(I)2.a(v,v)=\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}=\|v\|_{H^{1}(I)}^{2}. (3.51)

Thus it is coercive, and Theorem 1.2 applies. ∎

3.4.2 Pure periodic Poisson

We now consider

−u′′=fin ⁢I,u⁢(0)=u⁢(1),u′⁢(0)=u′⁢(1).-u^{\prime\prime}=f\quad\text{in }I,\qquad u(0)=u(1),\qquad u^{\prime}(0)=u^{% \prime}(1). (3.52)

Assume again that f∈L2⁢(I)f\in L^{2}(I). Here the bilinear form

a⁢(w,v)=∫01w′⁢v′⁢𝑑xa(w,v)=\int_{0}^{1}w^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,dx (3.53)

is not coercive on Hper1⁢(I)H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I) because constants belong to its kernel. We therefore introduce

Hper,⋆1⁢(I)={v∈Hper1⁢(I):∫01v⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=0}.H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I)=\left\{v\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I):\int_{0}^{1}% v(x)\,dx=0\right\}. (3.54)
Proposition 3.16.

Assume that

∫01f⁢(x)⁢𝑑x=0.\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx=0. (3.55)

Then there exists a unique function u∈Hper,⋆1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I) such that

∫01u′⁢v′⁢𝑑x=∫01f⁢v⁢𝑑xfor all ⁢v∈Hper,⋆1⁢(I).\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}fv\,dx\qquad\text{for all }v% \in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I). (3.56)

Any periodic weak solution is unique up to an additive constant.

Proof.

On Hper,⋆1⁢(I)H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I) the periodic Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality gives

‖v‖L2⁢(I)≤C⁢‖v′‖L2⁢(I).\|v\|_{L^{2}(I)}\leq C\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(I)}. (3.57)

Hence ‖v′‖L2⁢(I)\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(I)} is a norm on this space, aa is coercive, and the linear form is continuous. Theorem 1.2 yields existence and uniqueness in Hper,⋆1⁢(I)H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I).

To recover the original periodic problem, take any v∈Hper1⁢(I)v\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I) and write v=v⋆+v¯v=v_{\star}+\overline{v}, where v¯=∫01v⁢𝑑x\overline{v}=\int_{0}^{1}v\,dx and v⋆∈Hper,⋆1⁢(I)v_{\star}\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per},\star}(I). Compatibility gives

∫01u′⁢v′⁢𝑑x=∫01u′⁢v⋆′⁢𝑑x=∫01f⁢v⋆⁢𝑑x=∫01f⁢v⁢𝑑x.\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}u^{\prime}v_{\star}^{\prime}% \,dx=\int_{0}^{1}fv_{\star}\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}fv\,dx. (3.58)

Thus the identity holds for all periodic H1H^{1} test functions. Compactly supported tests give −u′′=f-u^{\prime\prime}=f in distributions, so u∈H2⁢(I)u\in H^{2}(I). Integration by parts then gives

0=u′⁢(1)⁢v⁢(1)−u′⁢(0)⁢v⁢(0)=(u′⁢(1)−u′⁢(0))⁢v⁢(0),0=u^{\prime}(1)v(1)-u^{\prime}(0)v(0)=(u^{\prime}(1)-u^{\prime}(0))v(0), (3.59)

and the common endpoint value of a periodic test function is arbitrary. Hence u′⁢(0)=u′⁢(1)u^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}(1); the other periodic condition already follows from u∈Hper1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{per}}(I). Testing the difference of two weak solutions against itself shows that the difference is constant. ∎

Remark 3.17.

The periodic case is the exact analogue of the pure Neumann Poisson problem. The kernel is again the space of constants, and the natural normalization is again the zero-mean condition.

3.5 A regularity complement for the Dirichlet model

The variational arguments above provide the weak solution in H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). For finite element error estimates, we often need one more derivative.

Proposition 3.18.

Consider the problem

−Δ⁢u+c⁢u=fin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω,-\Delta u+cu=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, (3.60)

with ff in L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega) and cc in L∞⁢(Ω)L^{\infty}(\Omega) satisfying c⁢(x)≥0c(x)\geq 0 almost everywhere. Assume that either ∂Ω\partial\Omega is sufficiently smooth (for instance of class C1,1C^{1,1}), or, in dimension two, that Ω\Omega is a convex polygon. Then the weak solution uu in H01⁢(Ω)H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) actually belongs to H2⁢(Ω)H^{2}(\Omega), and there exists a constant C>0C>0 such that

‖u‖H2⁢(Ω)≤C⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω).\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.61)

The constant depends on Ω\Omega, on ‖c‖L∞⁢(Ω)\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}, and on the coercivity constants of the Dirichlet problem.

Proof.

We only explain the reduction to the Poisson case. Since uu solves the weak problem, we have

∫Ω∇u⋅∇v⁢d⁢x+∫Ωc⁢u⁢v⁢𝑑x=∫Ωf⁢v⁢𝑑xfor all ⁢v∈H01⁢(Ω).\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\int_{\Omega}cuv\,dx=\int_{\Omega}fv\,% dx\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega). (3.62)

Taking v=uv=u and using c≥0c\geq 0 together with the Poincaré inequality, we obtain

‖u‖H01⁢(Ω)≤C⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω).\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.63)

In particular,

‖c⁢u‖L2⁢(Ω)≤‖c‖L∞⁢(Ω)⁢‖u‖L2⁢(Ω)≤C⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω).\left\|cu\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\|c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}% \left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. (3.64)

Hence g:=f−c⁢ug:=f-cu belongs to L2⁢(Ω)L^{2}(\Omega) and satisfies ‖g‖L2⁢(Ω)≤C⁢‖f‖L2⁢(Ω)\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}. The equation rewrites as

−Δ⁢u=gin ⁢Ω,u=0on ⁢∂Ω.-\Delta u=g\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. (3.65)

Applying the standard elliptic regularity result for the homogeneous Dirichlet Poisson problem yields u∈H2⁢(Ω)u\in H^{2}(\Omega) together with the stated estimate. ∎

Remark 3.19.

For the Poisson case c≡0c\equiv 0, the H2H^{2} estimate quoted in the proof is stated in [4, Lemma 4.6] under the assumption that ∂Ω\partial\Omega is sufficiently regular or that Ω\Omega is a convex polygon. For smooth domains, compare also [2, Proposition 4.18].

Why the extra derivative depends on geometry

In one dimension the Poisson regularity step is elementary: on I=(a,b)I=(a,b), the distributional identity u′′=−g∈L2⁢(I)u^{\prime\prime}=-g\in L^{2}(I), together with u∈H1⁢(I)u\in H^{1}(I), is precisely what is needed for u∈H2⁢(I)u\in H^{2}(I). Moreover,

‖u‖H2⁢(I)≤C⁢(‖u‖H1⁢(I)+‖g‖L2⁢(I)).\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(I)}\leq C\bigl{(}\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(I)}+\left\|g% \right\|_{L^{2}(I)}\bigr{)}. (3.66)

The energy estimate and g=f−c⁢ug=f-cu therefore prove Proposition 3.18 completely in dimension one.

In higher dimensions, Δ⁢u∈L2\Delta u\in L^{2} alone does not control each second derivative up to an arbitrary boundary. On a C1,1C^{1,1} domain, the standard proof first uses difference quotients to estimate second derivatives in the interior. Near the boundary it flattens the boundary and estimates tangential derivatives, using the homogeneous Dirichlet trace; the equation then controls the remaining normal derivative. A finite covering and localization give

‖u‖H2⁢(Ω)≤C⁢(‖g‖L2⁢(Ω)+‖u‖H1⁢(Ω)).\left\|u\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\bigl{(}\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}% +\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\bigr{)}. (3.67)

This is an outline of the Poisson regularity argument, not a replacement for the boundary estimates in the cited theorem. The reduction g=f−c⁢ug=f-cu above requires no derivatives of cc.

For a polygon, the role of convexity can be seen near a corner with interior angle ω\omega. In polar coordinates, the harmonic function

s⁢(r,θ)=rλ⁢sin⁡(λ⁢θ),λ=πω,0<θ<ω,s(r,\theta)=r^{\lambda}\sin(\lambda\theta),\qquad\lambda=\frac{\pi}{\omega},% \qquad 0<\theta<\omega, (3.68)

vanishes on both sides of the corner. Its second derivatives behave like rλ−2r^{\lambda-2}, so their squared integral near the corner is controlled by

∫0εr2⁢λ−3⁢𝑑r,\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}r^{2\lambda-3}\,dr, (3.69)

which is finite when λ>1\lambda>1. At every genuine corner of a convex polygon, ω<π\omega<\pi and hence λ>1\lambda>1. This explains the geometric hypothesis; a full proof additionally decomposes the solution into regular and corner parts. At a reentrant corner, ω>π\omega>\pi and λ<1\lambda<1: let w=χ⁢(r)⁢sw=\chi(r)s, where χ\chi is a smooth radial cutoff equal to one near the vertex and supported in a sufficiently small corner neighborhood. Then w∈H01⁢(Ω)w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega) and −Δ⁢w∈L2⁢(Ω)-\Delta w\in L^{2}(\Omega), but w∉H2⁢(Ω)w\notin H^{2}(\Omega). Thus smooth interior data alone do not imply the estimate on a nonconvex polygon.

Remark 3.20.

This regularity improvement is specific to the homogeneous Dirichlet setting under suitable geometric assumptions. For mixed boundary conditions, global H2H^{2} regularity is more delicate; singularities may appear near corners of ∂Ω\partial\Omega or near interface points where the boundary condition changes type, so the full H2H^{2} estimate may fail.
