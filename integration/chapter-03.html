Chapter 3
Outer measures and Carathéodory’s extension theorem

This chapter presents the classical extension procedure which starts from a set function defined on a small family of sets and produces an outer measure. The Carathéodory measurable sets then form a complete measure space, and the restriction to the generated σ-algebra gives the desired extension. The chapter complements the abstract material of the previous chapter, explains how the Lebesgue measure may also be constructed from rectangles by Carathéodory’s method, and introduces the Dynkin π-λ theorem and the associated uniqueness principle that will be used repeatedly later on.

3.1 Outer measures

Definition 3.1 (Outer measures). Let X be a set. An outer measure on X is a map

μ∗: P(X) → [0,∞]

such that

1.
μ∗(∅) = 0,
2.
if E ⊂ F ⊂ X, then μ∗(E) ≤ μ∗(F),
3.
for every sequence (En)n≥1 of subsets of X we have
μ∗ (⋃ n=1∞E n ) ≤∑ n=1∞μ∗(E n).

Proposition 3.2 (Outer measures from coverings). Let X be a set, let E be a family of subsets of X, and let m: E → [0,∞]. For every A ⊂ X, we define

μ∗(A) := inf ⁡ { ∑ j∈Jm(Ej) : J finite or countable,A ⊂⋃ j∈JEj,Ej ∈E },

where the empty family is allowed for the empty set, the corresponding sum is 0, and the infimum of the empty set is understood as + ∞. Then μ∗ is an outer measure on X.

Proof. We obviously have μ∗(∅) = 0, since the empty family covers ∅ with total cost 0. If A ⊂ B, then every covering of B by sets of E is also a covering of A, hence μ∗(A) ≤ μ∗(B). Let now (Ak)k≥1 be a sequence of subsets of X. If ∑ ⁡ k=1∞μ∗(A k) = +∞, then countable subadditivity is immediate. We may therefore assume that this sum is finite. Let 𝜀 > 0. For every k ≥ 1, we choose a finite or countable covering (Ek,j)j∈Jk of Ak by sets of E such that

∑ j∈Jkm(Ek,j) ≤ μ∗(A k) + 𝜀 2k.

Then the family (Ek,j)k≥1,j∈Jk is a countable covering of ⋃ ⁡ k≥1Ak, so

μ∗ (⋃ k=1∞A k ) ≤∑ k=1∞∑ j∈Jkm(Ek,j) ≤∑ k=1∞μ∗(A k) + 𝜀.

Letting 𝜀 → 0 yields the countable subadditivity. □

3.2 Carathéodory measurable sets

Definition 3.3 (Carathéodory measurable sets). Let μ∗ be an outer measure on a set X. A subset A of X is said to be μ∗-measurable in the sense of Carathéodory if, for every subset E of X,

μ∗(E) = μ∗(E ∩ A) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

Since subadditivity always gives

μ∗(E) ≤ μ∗(E ∩ A) + μ∗(E ∖ A),

Carathéodory measurability is equivalent to the reverse inequality for every subset E of X.

Theorem 3.4 (Caratheodory measurability theorem). Let μ∗ be an outer measure on a set X, and let

M := {A ⊂ X : A is μ∗-measurable}.

Then M is a σ-algebra on X, and the restriction of μ∗ to M is a complete measure.

Proof. Step 1. Elementary stability properties. We first note that X belongs to M, since for every E ⊂ X,

μ∗(E) = μ∗(E ∩ X) + μ∗(E ∖ X) = μ∗(E) + 0.

Moreover, if A belongs to M, then Ac also belongs to M, because

μ∗(E) = μ∗(E ∩ A) + μ∗(E ∖ A) = μ∗(E ∖ Ac) + μ∗(E ∩ Ac)

for every E ⊂ X.

Step 2. Finite intersections. We now prove that M is stable by finite intersections. Let A and B be in M, and let E ⊂ X. Since A is measurable, we have

μ∗(E) = μ∗(E ∩ A) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

We apply the measurability of B to the sets E ∩ A and E ∖ A:

μ∗(E ∩ A) = μ∗(E ∩ A ∩ B) + μ∗(E ∩ A ∖ B), μ∗(E ∖ A) = μ∗(E ∩ B ∖ A) + μ∗(E ∖ (A ∪ B)).

Hence

μ∗(E) = μ∗(E ∩ A ∩ B) + μ∗(E ∩ A ∖ B) + μ∗(E ∩ B ∖ A) + μ∗(E ∖ (A ∪ B)).

By countable subadditivity,

μ∗(E ∖ (A ∩ B)) ≤ μ∗(E ∩ A ∖ B) + μ∗(E ∩ B ∖ A) + μ∗(E ∖ (A ∪ B)).

Therefore

μ∗(E) ≥ μ∗(E ∩ A ∩ B) + μ∗(E ∖ (A ∩ B)).

The reverse inequality always follows from subadditivity, so A ∩ B is measurable. Since M is stable by complements and finite intersections, it is also stable by finite unions.

Step 3. Countable disjoint unions and additivity. We next prove that M is stable by countable disjoint unions, and that μ∗ is countably additive on such unions. Let (An)n≥1 be a sequence of pairwise disjoint sets in M, and let

A := ⋃ n=1∞A n.

For every E ⊂ X and every N ≥ 1, we use the measurability of A1,…,AN successively to obtain

μ∗(E) = ∑ n=1Nμ∗(E ∩ A n) + μ∗ (E ∖⋃ n=1NA n ).

Since

E ∖ A ⊂ E ∖⋃ n=1NA n,

monotonicity gives

μ∗(E) ≥∑ n=1Nμ∗(E ∩ A n) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

Letting N →∞, we obtain

μ∗(E) ≥∑ n=1∞μ∗(E ∩ A n) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

On the other hand,

E = (E ∩ A) ∪ (E ∖ A),

and

E ∩ A = ⋃ n=1∞(E ∩ A n)

is a disjoint union, so countable subadditivity yields

μ∗(E) ≤∑ n=1∞μ∗(E ∩ A n) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

Since

μ∗(E ∩ A) ≤∑ n=1∞μ∗(E ∩ A n),

we deduce that

μ∗(E) ≥ μ∗(E ∩ A) + μ∗(E ∖ A).

The reverse inequality follows from subadditivity, so A belongs to M. The two opposite inequalities above give equality. Taking E = A in this identity, and noting that A ∩ An = An, we obtain

μ∗(A) = ∑ n=1∞μ∗(A n).

Step 4. Arbitrary countable unions. Finally, let (An)n≥1 be an arbitrary sequence in M. We define

B1 := A1,Bn := An ∖⋃ k=1n−1A k(n ≥ 2).

Since M is stable by complements and finite intersections, every Bn belongs to M. The sets Bn are pairwise disjoint and

⋃ n=1∞A n = ⋃ n=1∞B n,

so the previous step shows that ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to M. Therefore M is a σ-algebra.

Step 5. Completeness. It remains to prove completeness. Let N ⊂ X be such that μ∗(N) = 0. For every E ⊂ X, we have

μ∗(E ∩ N) ≤ μ∗(N) = 0,

so

μ∗(E ∩ N) = 0.

Since

E = (E ∩ N) ∪ (E ∖ N),

subadditivity gives

μ∗(E) ≤ μ∗(E ∩ N) + μ∗(E ∖ N) = μ∗(E ∖ N).

The reverse inequality follows from monotonicity, hence N belongs to M. In particular, if M belongs to M, μ∗(M) = 0, and N ⊂ M, then μ∗(N) = 0, hence N belongs to M. Thus the restricted measure is complete in the usual sense. □

3.3 Extension from a premeasure

Definition 3.5 (Algebras and premeasures). Let X be a set.

1.
A family E ⊂P(X) is called an algebra of sets if X belongs to E, and if E is stable by finite unions and complements. An algebra is then automatically stable by finite intersections and by set differences.
2.
A map μ0: E → [0,∞] is called a premeasure if μ0(∅) = 0 and if, for every sequence (En)n≥1 of pairwise disjoint sets in E such that ⋃ ⁡ n≥1En still belongs to E, we have
μ0 ( ⋃ n=1∞E n ) = ∑ n=1∞μ 0(En).

Theorem 3.6 (Caratheodory extension theorem). Let X be a set, let E be an algebra of subsets of X, and let μ0 be a premeasure on E. We define an outer measure μ∗ on X by

μ∗(A) := inf ⁡ { ∑ n=1∞μ 0(En): A ⊂⋃ n=1∞E n,En ∈E },

where finite coverings may be padded by empty sets, and the empty set is covered by the constant sequence En = ∅. Then every set of E is μ∗-measurable, and

μ∗(E) = μ 0(E)

for every E in E. Consequently, the restriction of μ∗ to σ(E) is a measure extending μ0.

Proof. Step 1. Measurability of the algebra. The previous proposition shows that μ∗ is an outer measure. We shall use the elementary consequences that a premeasure on an algebra is finitely additive, monotone, and finitely subadditive. Finite additivity follows by applying the definition of premeasure to a finite disjoint family completed by empty sets; monotonicity and finite subadditivity then follow as in Theorem 2.2. Let A be in E, and let B ⊂ X. For every covering

B ⊂⋃ n=1∞E n,En ∈E,

we have

B ∩ A ⊂⋃ n=1∞(E n ∩ A),B ∖ A ⊂⋃ n=1∞(E n ∖ A),

and all the sets En ∩ A and En ∖ A belong to E. Moreover,

En = (En ∩ A) ∪ (En ∖ A)

is a disjoint union, so finite additivity on the algebra gives

μ0(En) = μ0(En ∩ A) + μ0(En ∖ A).

Therefore

μ∗(B ∩ A) + μ∗(B ∖ A) ≤∑ n=1∞μ 0(En).

Taking the infimum over all coverings of B, we obtain

μ∗(B ∩ A) + μ∗(B ∖ A) ≤ μ∗(B).

The converse inequality follows from subadditivity, so A is μ∗-measurable.

Step 2. Equality on the algebra. We now prove that μ∗(A) = μ 0(A). The inequality μ∗(A) ≤ μ 0(A) is immediate, since A covers itself. Conversely, let

A ⊂⋃ n=1∞E n,En ∈E.

For every N ≥ 1, we define

CN := A ∩⋃ n=1NE n.

Then CN belongs to E, the sequence (CN)N≥1 is increasing, and

⋃ N=1∞C N = A.

We write

D1 := C1,DN := CN ∖ CN−1(N ≥ 2).

The sets DN belong to E, they are pairwise disjoint, and

A = ⋃ N=1∞D N.

Since A belongs to E and μ0 is a premeasure,

μ0(A) = ∑ N=1∞μ 0(DN) = lim ⁡ N→∞μ0(CN).

For each N, we have

CN ⊂⋃ n=1N(A ∩ E n),

so finite subadditivity on the algebra yields

μ0(CN) ≤∑ n=1Nμ 0(A ∩ En) ≤∑ n=1Nμ 0(En).

Letting N →∞, we obtain

μ0(A) ≤∑ n=1∞μ 0(En).

Taking the infimum over all coverings of A gives μ0(A) ≤ μ∗(A). Hence μ∗(A) = μ 0(A).

Step 3. Restriction to the generated σ-algebra. We have proved that E ⊂M, where M denotes the Carathéodory σ-algebra associated with μ∗. Since M is a σ-algebra, it contains σ(E). Therefore the restriction of μ∗ to σ(E) is a measure extending μ0. □

3.4 Dynkin systems and uniqueness on generators

Definition 3.7 (Dynkin systems). Let X be a set.

1.
A family D ⊂P(X) is called a Dynkin system if
(a)
X belongs to D,
(b)
if A and B belong to D and A ⊂ B, then B ∖ A belongs to D,
(c)
if (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in D, then ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to D.
2.
A family P ⊂P(X) is called a π-system if it is stable by finite intersections.

Remark. This definition is equivalent to the common definition in which a Dynkin system is required to be stable under countable disjoint unions. Indeed, increasing unions follow by taking partial disjoint unions, and conversely the difference axiom turns an increasing union into a countable disjoint union of successive differences.

Theorem 3.8 (Dynkin pi-lambda theorem). Let P be a π-system on a set X. Then σ(P) is the smallest Dynkin system containing P.

Proof. Step 1. The minimal Dynkin system. Let D be the smallest Dynkin system containing P; it is obtained as the intersection of all Dynkin systems containing P.

We first prove that, for every A in P, the class

DA := {B ⊂ X : A ∩ B ∈D}

is a Dynkin system. Since A ∩ X = A belongs to P ⊂D, we have X ∈DA. If B and C belong to DA and B ⊂ C, then

A ∩ (C ∖ B) = (A ∩ C) ∖ (A ∩ B),

so C ∖ B belongs to DA because D is a Dynkin system. If (Bn)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in DA, then

A ∩⋃ n=1∞B n = ⋃ n=1∞(A ∩ B n),

and the sequence (A ∩ Bn)n≥1 is increasing in D. Hence ⋃ ⁡ n≥1Bn belongs to DA. Thus DA is a Dynkin system.

Since P is a π-system, for every B in P we have

A ∩ B ∈P ⊂D,

so P ⊂DA. By minimality of D, it follows that D ⊂DA. Therefore, for every A in P and every B in D, we have

A ∩ B ∈D.

Step 2. Stability under intersections with elements of D. We now consider the class

E := {A ⊂ X : A ∩ B ∈D for every B ∈D}.

We claim that E is a Dynkin system. Since X ∩ B = B belongs to D for every B in D, we have X ∈E. If A and C belong to E and A ⊂ C, then for every B in D,

(C ∖ A) ∩ B = (C ∩ B) ∖ (A ∩ B),

and the right-hand side belongs to D because D is a Dynkin system. Hence C ∖ A belongs to E. If (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in E, then for every B in D,

(⋃ n=1∞A n) ∩ B = ⋃ n=1∞(A n ∩ B),

and the sequence (An ∩ B)n≥1 is increasing in D. Therefore ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to E. Thus E is a Dynkin system.

The first step shows that P ⊂E. By minimality of D, we obtain D ⊂E. Hence, if A and B belong to D, then

A ∩ B ∈D.

So D is stable by finite intersections.

Step 3. The Dynkin system is a σ-algebra. A Dynkin system is stable by complements, because

X ∖ A ∈D

whenever A belongs to D. Since D is also stable by finite intersections, it is stable by finite unions. If (An)n≥1 is any sequence in D, the partial unions

UN := ⋃ n=1NA n

belong to D and form an increasing sequence. Hence

⋃ n=1∞A n = ⋃ N=1∞U N

belongs to D. Therefore D is a σ-algebra.

Step 4. Identification with σ(P). Since D contains P, we have

σ(P) ⊂D.

Conversely, every σ-algebra is a Dynkin system, so the minimality of D gives

D ⊂ σ(P).

Thus D = σ(P). □

Definition 3.9 (Monotone classes). Let X be a set. A family M ⊂P(X) is called a monotone class if

1.
whenever (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in M, then ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to M,
2.
whenever (An)n≥1 is a decreasing sequence in M, then ⋂ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to M.

Theorem 3.10 (Monotone class theorem). Let E be an algebra of subsets of a set X. Then σ(E) is the smallest monotone class containing E.

Proof. Step 1. Stability under complements. Let M be the smallest monotone class containing E; it is obtained as the intersection of all monotone classes containing E. We first prove that M is stable by complements. Let

C := {A ∈M: Ac ∈M}.

Since E is an algebra, we have E ⊂C. If (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in C, then A := ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to M and

Ac = ⋂ n=1∞A nc

belongs to M because (Anc) n≥1 is decreasing in M. Thus A belongs to C. Similarly, if (An)n≥1 is a decreasing sequence in C, then A := ⋂ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to M and

Ac = ⋃ n=1∞A nc

belongs to M because (Anc) n≥1 is increasing in M. Hence A belongs to C. Therefore C is a monotone class containing E, so by minimality of M we obtain

C = M.

Thus M is stable by complements.

Step 2. Intersections with elements of the algebra. We next prove that M is stable by finite intersections. For every A in E, we define

MA := {B ∈M: A ∩ B ∈M}.

Since E is an algebra, if B belongs to E, then A ∩ B also belongs to E ⊂M. Hence E ⊂MA. If (Bn)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in MA, then

A ∩⋃ n=1∞B n = ⋃ n=1∞(A ∩ B n)

belongs to M, because (A ∩ Bn)n≥1 is increasing in M. Thus ⋃ ⁡ n≥1Bn belongs to MA. Similarly, if (Bn)n≥1 is a decreasing sequence in MA, then

A ∩⋂ n=1∞B n = ⋂ n=1∞(A ∩ B n)

belongs to M. Hence MA is a monotone class containing E, so MA = M. Therefore, for every A in E and every B in M, we have A ∩ B in M.

Step 3. Intersections of arbitrary elements of M. We now define

N := {A ∈M: A ∩ B ∈M for every B ∈M}.

The previous step gives E ⊂N. If (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in N and if B belongs to M, then

(⋃ n=1∞A n) ∩ B = ⋃ n=1∞(A n ∩ B)

belongs to M. Similarly, if (An)n≥1 is a decreasing sequence in N, then

(⋂ n=1∞A n) ∩ B = ⋂ n=1∞(A n ∩ B)

belongs to M. Hence N is a monotone class containing E, so N = M. Thus M is stable by finite intersections.

Step 4. Identification with the generated σ-algebra. Since M is stable by complements and finite intersections, it is stable by finite unions. If (An)n≥1 is any sequence in M, then the partial unions

UN := ⋃ n=1NA n

belong to M and form an increasing sequence. Therefore

⋃ n=1∞A n = ⋃ N=1∞U N

belongs to M. So M is a σ-algebra containing E, hence

σ(E) ⊂M.

Conversely, every σ-algebra is a monotone class, so the minimality of M yields

M ⊂ σ(E).

Thus M = σ(E). □

Corollary 3.11 (Approximation by a generating algebra). Let (X,A,μ) be a finite measure space, and let E be an algebra of subsets of X such that

σ(E) = A.

Then for every E in A and every η > 0, there exists A in E such that

μ(E△A) < η.

Proof. We define

D := {E ∈A : ∀ ⁡η > 0,∃ ⁡A ∈E such that μ(E△A) < η}.

Clearly E ⊂D.

We claim that D is a monotone class. Let (En)n≥1 be an increasing sequence in D, and set

E := ⋃ n=1∞E n.

Since μ(X) < ∞, continuity from below gives

μ(E ∖ En) → 0as n → +∞.

Given η > 0, we choose n such that

μ(E ∖ En) < η∕2.

Since En belongs to D, there exists A in E such that

μ(En△A) < η∕2.

Then

μ(E△A) ≤ μ(E ∖ En) + μ(En△A) < η.

Hence E belongs to D.

If (En)n≥1 is decreasing in D and

E := ⋂ n=1∞E n,

then continuity from above gives

μ(En ∖ E) → 0as n → +∞

because μ(E1) ≤ μ(X) < ∞. Given η > 0, choose n such that μ(En ∖ E) < η∕2. Since En belongs to D, choose A in E such that μ(En△A) < η∕2. Then

μ(E△A) ≤ μ(En ∖ E) + μ(En△A) < η.

Thus E belongs to D. Thus D is a monotone class.

Since D is a monotone class containing the algebra E, the monotone class theorem (Theorem 3.10) yields

A = σ(E) ⊂D.

This proves the corollary. □

Corollary 3.12 (Uniqueness on a pi-system). Let P be a π-system on a set X such that X belongs to P. Let μ and ν be finite measures on σ(P). If

μ(E) = ν(E)

for every E in P, then μ = ν on σ(P).

Proof. We consider the class

D := {A ∈ σ(P): μ(A) = ν(A)}.

Since X belongs to P, we have

μ(X) = ν(X),

so X belongs to D. If A and B belong to D and A ⊂ B, then

μ(B ∖ A) = μ(B) − μ(A) = ν(B) − ν(A) = ν(B ∖ A),

so B ∖ A belongs to D. If (An)n≥1 is an increasing sequence in D, continuity from below gives

μ (⋃ n=1∞A n) = lim ⁡ n→∞μ(An) = lim ⁡ n→∞ν(An) = ν (⋃ n=1∞A n) ,

so ⋃ ⁡ n≥1An belongs to D. Thus D is a Dynkin system. Since it contains P, Theorem 3.8 implies that

D = σ(P).

Hence μ = ν on σ(P). □

Lemma 3.13 (Half-open intervals as a pi-system). Let J be a bounded interval of ℝ. Define

PJ := {∅,J}∪{(s,t] ⊂ J : s < t}.

Then PJ is a π-system on J and

σ(PJ) = B(J).

Proof. The intersection of two intervals of the form (s,t] is either empty or again of the same form, and the empty set belongs to PJ. Intersections with J do not leave the family. Thus PJ is a π-system. Every element of PJ is Borel in J, hence σ(PJ) ⊂B(J). Conversely, for every a < b with (a,b) ⊂ J, we have

(a,b) = ⋃ n≥1 (a,b −1 n ]

where terms with b − 1∕n ≤ a are simply omitted. Hence every open interval contained in J belongs to σ(PJ). Since the open intervals contained in J generate the Borel σ-algebra of J, we obtain B(J) ⊂ σ(PJ). □

Corollary 3.14 (Uniqueness of σ-finite extensions). Let E be an algebra on X, and let μ0 be a premeasure on E. Assume that μ0 is σ-finite on E, that is, that there exists a sequence (En)n≥1 in E such that

X = ⋃ n=1∞E n,μ0(En) < ∞

for every n ≥ 1. Then the extension of μ0 to σ(E) is unique.

Proof. Let μ and ν be two measures on σ(E) extending μ0. Replacing En by

Fn := ⋃ k=1nE k,

we may assume that the sequence (En)n≥1 is increasing.

For every n ≥ 1, we set

Pn := {A ∩ En: A ∈E}.

Since E is an algebra, Pn is a π-system on En and En belongs to Pn. Let

An := {B ∩ En: B ∈ σ(E)}.

Then An is a σ-algebra on En containing Pn, so

σ(Pn) ⊂An.

Conversely, the class

Gn := {B ∈ σ(E): B ∩ En ∈ σ(Pn)}

is a σ-algebra on X which contains E. Hence

Gn = σ(E),

which means that

An ⊂ σ(Pn).

Therefore

An = σ(Pn).

We now define two finite measures on An by

μn(C) := μ(C),νn(C) := ν(C)

for every C in An. Since μ and ν extend μ0, for every A in E we have

μn(A ∩ En) = μ(A ∩ En) = μ0(A ∩ En) = ν(A ∩ En) = νn(A ∩ En).

Thus μn and νn coincide on Pn. Corollary 3.12 applied on En yields

μ(B ∩ En) = ν(B ∩ En)

for every B in σ(E).

Since En ↑ X, continuity from below gives, for every B in σ(E),

μ(B) = lim ⁡ n→∞μ(B ∩ En) = lim ⁡ n→∞ν(B ∩ En) = ν(B).

Hence μ = ν on σ(E). □

3.5 Application to the Lebesgue measure

For N ≥ 1, let ℐ be the family consisting of ∅, ℝ, and all intervals of the forms

(a,b],(−∞,b],(a, +∞),

where a,b are real numbers and a < b. Let EN be the family of all finite disjoint unions of rectangles

R = I1 ×⋯ × IN,Ij ∈ ℐ .

This family is an algebra on ℝN: finite intersections of such rectangles are rectangles of the same type, and complements are finite disjoint unions of such rectangles.

For I in ℐ , define

ℓ(∅) = 0,ℓ((a,b]) = b − a,ℓ((−∞,b]) = ℓ((a, +∞)) = ℓ(ℝ) = +∞.

For a rectangle R = I1 ×⋯ × IN, set

|R| := ∏ j=1Nℓ(I j),

with the conventions of Section 1.4. If E in EN is written as a finite disjoint union

E = ⋃ j=1mR j,

we define

μ0(E) := ∑ j=1m|R j|.

The finite-refinement argument shows that this definition is independent of the chosen disjoint representation. Indeed, two finite decompositions admit the common refinement formed by all nonempty intersections of rectangles from the two decompositions, and the volume of a rectangle is the sum of the volumes of the pieces obtained by cutting its sides at finitely many endpoints. The same refinement argument gives finite additivity on EN.

The map μ0 is a premeasure on EN. For finite-volume sets this follows from the standard compactness argument for rectangles: if E = ⋃ ⁡ k≥1Ek is a disjoint union in EN, with E also in EN, then finite additivity gives ∑ ⁡ k=1mμ 0(Ek) ≤ μ0(E) for every m, and the reverse inequality is obtained by shrinking the finitely many rectangles in a decomposition of E and enlarging the finitely many rectangles in decompositions of the Ek, then extracting a finite subcover. The infinite-volume case follows by intersecting with the boxes (−r,r]N and letting r → +∞.

The Carathéodory extension theorem therefore yields a measure on σ(EN) extending this notion of volume. Since every element of EN is Borel, we have σ(EN) ⊂B(ℝN). Conversely, bounded half-open rectangles with rational endpoints belong to EN and generate the Euclidean Borel σ-algebra. Hence

σ(EN) = B(ℝN).

This produces the classical Borel Lebesgue measure and proves the existence part of Theorem 2.10. Later, in Section 14.3, we will recover the same Borel measure from the Riesz representation theorem and then describe its completion.

3.6 Stieltjes measures and absolute continuity of functions

The phrase “absolute continuity” has already been used for measures in Section 2.5. We now record the corresponding notion for functions on a compact interval and explain the link between the two notions through Stieltjes measures. This material will be used later in the proof of the converse part of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Definition 3.15 (Absolutely continuous functions on a compact interval). Let a < b be real numbers and let F : [a,b] → ℝ be a function. We say that F is absolutely continuous on [a,b] if, for every 𝜀 > 0, there exists δ > 0 such that, for every finite family of pairwise disjoint intervals (αk,βk) with

a ≤ αk < βk ≤ b,

if

∑ k=1m(β k − αk) < δ,

then

∑ k=1m|F(β k) − F(αk)| < 𝜀.

We denote by AC([a,b]) the set of absolutely continuous functions on [a,b].

Proposition 3.16 (Basic properties of absolutely continuous functions). Let a < b be real numbers. Every function in AC([a,b]) is continuous on [a,b]. Moreover, AC([a,b]) is a real vector space for the pointwise operations.

Proof. Let F be in AC([a,b]). Let 𝜀 > 0 and choose δ > 0 as in Definition 3.15. If x,y are in [a,b] and |x − y| < δ, then, assuming for instance that x < y, the family consisting of the single interval (x,y) gives

|F(y) − F(x)| < 𝜀.

Thus F is uniformly continuous, and in particular continuous.

Let F and G be in AC([a,b]). For F + G, given 𝜀 > 0, choose numbers δF > 0 and δG > 0 associated respectively with F and G and with the tolerance 𝜀∕2. If

∑ k=1m(β k − αk) < min ⁡(δF ,δG),

then

∑ k=1m|(F + G)(β k) − (F + G)(αk)| ≤∑ k=1m|F(β k) − F(αk)| + ∑ k=1m|G(β k) − G(αk)| < 𝜀.

Hence F + G is absolutely continuous.

Let c be in ℝ. If c = 0, then cF is absolutely continuous. If c≠0, we choose δ > 0 associated with F and with the tolerance 𝜀∕|c|. Then

∑ k=1m|cF(β k) − cF(αk)| < 𝜀

whenever the total length of the intervals is smaller than δ. Thus cF is absolutely continuous. Therefore AC([a,b]) is a real vector space. □

Theorem 3.17 (Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure on a compact interval). Let a < b be real numbers and let F : [a,b] → ℝ be increasing and right-continuous at every point of [a,b[. Then there exists a unique finite Borel measure μF on [a,b] such that

μF ({a}) = 0

and, for every a ≤ s < t ≤ b,

μF ((s,t]) = F(t) − F(s).

The measure μF is called the Stieltjes measure associated with F.

Proof. Let E be the algebra of subsets of [a,b] consisting of finite disjoint unions of the singleton {a} and intervals of the form (s,t], where a ≤ s < t ≤ b. On this algebra, define μ0 by

μ0({a}) = 0,μ0((s,t]) = F(t) − F(s),

and extend this formula additively to finite disjoint unions. The usual finite-refinement argument shows that μ0 is well defined and finitely additive.

We prove that μ0 is a premeasure. Since μ0([a,b]) = F(b) − F(a) < +∞, it is enough to prove continuity from above at the empty set. Let (An)n≥1 be a decreasing sequence in E such that

⋂ n=1∞A n = ∅.

Fix 𝜀 > 0. For every n ≥ 1, we choose a compact set Kn ⊂ An and a set Bn in E such that

An ∖ Kn ⊂ Bn,μ0(Bn) < 𝜀2−n.

This is obtained as follows. Write An as a finite disjoint union of a possible singleton {a} and half-open intervals (s,t]. The singleton is kept in the compact part. On each interval (s,t], we approximate from inside by [s + r,t] and cover the discarded part by (s,s + r]. Since F is right-continuous at s, the quantity

μ0((s,s + r]) = F(s + r) − F(s)

can be made arbitrarily small. Since the decomposition is finite, the discarded pieces can be chosen so that their total μ0-mass is smaller than 𝜀2−n.

Since ⋂ ⁡ n≥1Kn = ∅ and the sets Kn are compact subsets of [a,b], compactness gives a finite set of indices n1,…,nq such that

Kn1 ∩⋯ ∩ Knq = ∅.

Let N := max ⁡(n1,… ⁡,nq). Since the sequence (An) is decreasing, if x belongs to AN, then x belongs to every Anj. If x belonged to none of the sets Bnj, the inclusions Anj ∖ Knj ⊂ Bnj would imply x ∈ Knj for every j, contradicting the emptiness of Kn1 ∩⋯ ∩ Knq. Hence

AN ⊂⋃ j=1qB nj.

Since AN and the Bnj belong to E, finite subadditivity gives

μ0(AN) ≤∑ j=1qμ 0(Bnj) < 𝜀.

Thus μ0(An) → 0, so μ0 is a finite premeasure on E.

The Carathéodory extension theorem gives a finite measure on σ(E). Since the intervals (s,t] and the singleton {a} generate the Borel σ-algebra of [a,b], this gives a finite Borel measure μF with the desired properties.

The uniqueness follows from Corollary 3.12, applied to the π-system

P := {[a,t] : a ≤ t ≤ b}.

This π-system contains [a,b] and generates B([a,b]). The prescribed identities determine the value of the measure on P, since

μF ([a,t]) = μF ({a}) + μF ((a,t]) = F(t) − F(a).
□

Proposition 3.18 (Absolute continuity and the associated Stieltjes measure). Let a < b be real numbers and let F : [a,b] → ℝ be increasing and right-continuous at every point of [a,b[. Let μF be the associated Stieltjes measure. Then

F ∈ AC([a,b])⟺μF ≪ λ1|[a,b].

Proof. Assume first that F is absolutely continuous. By Proposition 3.16, F is continuous on [a,b]. Let E be a Borel subset of [a,b] such that λ1(E) = 0, and let 𝜀 > 0. Choose δ > 0 in the definition of absolute continuity of F associated with 𝜀. By the outer-measure construction of the Lebesgue measure, there exists an open set U in ℝ such that

E ⊂ U,λ1(U) < δ.

Write U as a countable disjoint union of open intervals. Intersecting these intervals with ]a,b] and replacing each nonempty intersection by the corresponding half-open interval of the form (αj,βj] gives a countable disjoint family such that

E∩]a,b] ⊂⋃ j(αj,βj],∑ j(βj − αj) ≤ λ1(U) < δ.

The point a has μF -measure zero by the definition of the Stieltjes measure. Therefore, for every finite subset J of indices, the absolute continuity of F gives

∑ j∈JμF ((αj,βj]) = ∑ j∈J (F(βj) − F(αj) ) < 𝜀.

By countable additivity, equivalently by continuity from below for measures, the whole countable sum is at most 𝜀, and hence μF (E) ≤ 𝜀. Since 𝜀 is arbitrary, μF (E) = 0. Thus μF ≪ λ1|[a,b].

Conversely, assume that μF ≪ λ1|[a,b]. Since both measures are finite, Lemma 2.17 gives, for every 𝜀 > 0, a number δ > 0 such that

λ1(A) < δ⟹μF (A) < 𝜀

for every Borel set A ⊂ [a,b]. Let (αk,βk)1≤k≤m be a finite family of pairwise disjoint intervals with a ≤ αk < βk ≤ b and

∑ k=1m(β k − αk) < δ.

Set

A := ⋃ k=1m(α k,βk].

Then λ1(A) < δ, and therefore

∑ k=1m|F(β k) − F(αk)| = ∑ k=1m (F(β k) − F(αk) ) = μF (A) < 𝜀.

Thus F is absolutely continuous on [a,b]. □
