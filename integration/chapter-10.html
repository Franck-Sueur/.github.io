Chapter 10
Product measures, the Fubini and Tonelli theorems

The basic method for calculating an integral of a function of two variables is to reduce it to iterated integrals of functions of a single variable. To do so, we first have to explain how to put a measure-space structure on a Cartesian product.

10.1 Definition of σ-finite measures

Definition 10.1 (σ-finite measures). Let (X,A,μ) be a measure space. We say that (X,A,μ) is σ-finite if there exists a sequence (An)n≥1 of elements of A such that

μ(An) < ∞for every n ≥ 1,X = ⋃ n≥1An.

This hypothesis is satisfied, for instance, when μ(X) < ∞, when X = ℕ is endowed with the discrete σ-algebra and the counting measure, or when X = ℝn is endowed with the Lebesgue measure.

10.2 σ-algebra tensor product and measure tensor product

Definition 10.2 (Product σ-algebra). Let (X1,A1) and (X2,A2) be two measurable spaces. Let

R := {B1 × B2: B1 ∈A1,B2 ∈A2}.

The product σ-algebra is

A1 ⊗A2 := σ(R).

Proposition 10.3 (Product measure). Let (X1,A1,μ1) and (X2,A2,μ2) be two σ-finite measure spaces. Then there exists a unique measure, denoted by μ1 ⊗ μ2, on A1 ⊗A2 such that

(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(B1 × B2) = μ1(B1)μ2(B2)

for all B1 in A1 and all B2 in A2. Moreover, the measure space (X1 × X2,A1 ⊗A2,μ1 ⊗ μ2) is σ-finite.

Proof. Step 1. The algebra generated by rectangles. Let

R := {B1 × B2: B1 ∈A1,B2 ∈A2}.

Since

(B1 × B2) ∩ (C1 × C2) = (B1 ∩ C1) × (B2 ∩ C2),

the intersection of two rectangles is again a rectangle. Moreover, for two rectangles B1 × B2 and C1 × C2, we have the disjoint decomposition

(B1 × B2) ∖ (C1 × C2) = ((B1 ∖ C1) × B2) ∪ ((B1 ∩ C1) × (B2 ∖ C2)).

It follows by induction that the difference between a rectangle and a finite disjoint union of rectangles is again a finite disjoint union of rectangles. Consequently, every finite union of rectangles can be rewritten as a finite disjoint union of rectangles. Finally, since

(X1 × X2) ∖ (B1 × B2) = ((X1 ∖ B1) × X2) ∪ (B1 × (X2 ∖ B2)),

and since complements of finite disjoint unions can be treated recursively by repeated use of the previous difference formula, the family E of finite disjoint unions of elements of R is an algebra on X1 × X2. It contains R, hence

σ(E) = σ(R) = A1 ⊗A2.

Step 2. Definition of the premeasure through sections. Let E be in E. We choose a disjoint decomposition

E = ⋃ i=1m(B 1,i × B2,i),B1,i ∈A1,B2,i ∈A2.

For every x in X1, we define the vertical section

Ex := {y ∈ X2: (x,y) ∈ E}.

Because the rectangles are pairwise disjoint, the sets B2,i such that x belongs to B1,i are pairwise disjoint. Therefore

μ2(Ex) = ∑ i=1mμ 2(B2,i)1B1,i(x).

In particular, the map x↦μ2(Ex) is measurable. We then set

μ0(E) := ∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x).

This quantity only depends on E, since it is defined from the sections Ex. If E = B1 × B2, then

Ex = { B2if x ∈ B1, ∅ if x∉B1,

so

μ0(B1 × B2) = ∫ X1μ2(B2)1B1(x)dμ1(x) = μ1(B1)μ2(B2),

with the usual convention 0 ⋅∞ = 0.

Step 3. Countable additivity on the algebra. Let now (En)n≥1 be a sequence of pairwise disjoint elements of E, and assume that

E := ⋃ n=1∞E n

still belongs to E. For every x in X1, we have

Ex = ⋃ n=1∞(E n)x,

and this union is disjoint. Hence

μ2(Ex) = ∑ n=1∞μ 2((En)x).

By the monotone convergence theorem,

μ0(E) = ∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1 ∑ n=1∞μ 2((En)x)dμ1(x) = ∑ n=1∞∫ X1μ2((En)x)dμ1(x) = ∑ n=1∞μ 0(En).

Thus μ0 is a premeasure on E.

Step 4. Extension and uniqueness. Since μ1 and μ2 are σ-finite, there exist increasing sequences (X1,n)n≥1 in A1 and (X2,n)n≥1 in A2 such that

X1 = ⋃ n=1∞X 1,n,X2 = ⋃ n=1∞X 2,n,

and

μ1(X1,n) < ∞,μ2(X2,n) < ∞

for every n ≥ 1. Then the sets

Cn := X1,n × X2,n

belong to E, satisfy

μ0(Cn) = μ1(X1,n)μ2(X2,n) < ∞,

and form an increasing covering of X1 × X2. Hence the premeasure μ0 is σ-finite on E. By the Carathéodory extension theorem, it extends to a measure on

σ(E) = A1 ⊗A2.

By the uniqueness result proved in Chapter 3, this extension is unique. It is denoted by μ1 ⊗ μ2. The same sequence (Cn) shows that (X1 × X2,A1 ⊗A2,μ1 ⊗ μ2) is σ-finite. □

In the case of the Borel σ-algebras of Euclidean spaces, we record the following consequence.

Proposition 10.4 (Euclidean product structures). For all integers n,m ≥ 1, we have

B(ℝn+m) = B(ℝn) ⊗B(ℝm).

Moreover, if λd denotes the Lebesgue measure on ℝd, then

λn+m = λn ⊗ λm.

Proof. The projections from ℝn+m = ℝn × ℝm onto the two factors are continuous. Hence every set A × B, with A in B(ℝn) and B in B(ℝm), is Borel in ℝn+m, and therefore

B(ℝn) ⊗B(ℝm) ⊂B(ℝn+m).

Conversely, every open subset of ℝn+m is a countable union of products U × V , where U and V are open rectangles with rational endpoints in ℝn and ℝm. Such products belong to B(ℝn) ⊗B(ℝm), so

B(ℝn+m) ⊂B(ℝn) ⊗B(ℝm).

This proves the equality of the Borel σ-algebras.

For the measures, both λn+m and λn ⊗ λm are σ-finite Borel measures on ℝn+m. On every half-open rectangle R × S, with R a half-open rectangle of ℝn and S a half-open rectangle of ℝm, the construction of Lebesgue measure gives

λn+m(R × S) = λn(R)λm(S) = (λn ⊗ λm)(R × S).

These rectangles form a generating π-system. By uniqueness of the σ-finite extension, the two measures coincide. □

10.3 The Tonelli theorem for positive measurable functions

Theorem 10.5 (Tonelli theorem). Let (X1,A1,μ1) and (X2,A2,μ2) be two σ-finite measure spaces. Let

f : X1 × X2 → [0,∞]

be an A1 ⊗A2-measurable function. Then

1.
for every x in X1, the section y↦f(x,y) is (A2,B([0,∞]))-measurable, and the map
x↦∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y)

is (A1,B([0,∞]))-measurable,

2.
for every y in X2, the section x↦f(x,y) is (A1,B([0,∞]))-measurable, and the map
y↦∫ X1f(x,y)dμ1(x)

is (A2,B([0,∞]))-measurable,

3.
we have
∫ X1×X2f(x,y)d(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(x,y) = ∫ X1 (∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y)) dμ1(x) = ∫ X2 (∫ X1f(x,y)dμ1(x)) dμ2(y).

Proof. For each fixed x in X1, the map y↦(x,y) from X2 to X1 × X2 is measurable: the preimage of a rectangle A × B is B if x is in A and is ∅ otherwise, and the rectangles generate the product σ-algebra. Hence the section y↦f(x,y) is measurable. The same argument gives the measurability of the sections x↦f(x,y) for fixed y in X2. We therefore only have to prove the measurability of the partial integrals and the identity of the integrals.

Step 1: the finite case. Assume first that μ1(X1) < ∞ and μ2(X2) < ∞. For every E in A1 ⊗A2, we write

Ex := {y ∈ X2: (x,y) ∈ E},Ey := {x ∈ X 1: (x,y) ∈ E}.

Let C be the class of sets E in A1 ⊗A2 such that

1.
the maps x↦μ2(Ex) and y↦μ1(Ey) are measurable,
2.
(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(E) = ∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x) = ∫ X2μ1(Ey)dμ 2(y).

We apply Theorem 3.8. We first check that C is a Dynkin system. Since both measures are finite, the whole space X1 × X2 belongs to C. Now let E,F in C with E ⊂ F. Then

(F ∖ E)x = Fx ∖ Ex,(F ∖ E)y = Fy ∖ Ey,

hence

μ2((F ∖ E)x) = μ2(Fx) − μ2(Ex),μ1((F ∖ E)y) = μ 1(Fy) − μ 1(Ey).

These maps are measurable, and

∫ X1μ2((F ∖ E)x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1μ2(Fx)dμ1(x) −∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x) = (μ1 ⊗ μ2)(F) − (μ1 ⊗ μ2)(E) = (μ1 ⊗ μ2)(F ∖ E).

The same computation with the horizontal sections shows that F ∖ E belongs to C.

Let now (En)n≥1 be an increasing sequence in C, and set

E := ⋃ n≥1En.

For every x in X1, we have

(En)x ↑ Ex,

hence

μ2((En)x) ↑ μ2(Ex).

Therefore x↦μ2(Ex) is measurable as the increasing limit of measurable functions, and the monotone convergence theorem gives

∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞∫ X1μ2((En)x)dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(En) = (μ1 ⊗ μ2)(E).

The same argument with the horizontal sections shows that E belongs to C. Thus C is a Dynkin system.

If E = A × B with A in A1 and B in A2, then

Ex = { Bif x ∈ A, ∅ if  x∉ A, Ey = { Aif y ∈ B, ∅if y∉B.

Therefore

x↦μ2(Ex) = μ2(B)1A(x),y↦μ1(Ey) = μ 1(A)1B(y),

and

∫ X1μ2(Ex)dμ1(x) = μ2(B)μ1(A) = (μ1 ⊗ μ2)(A × B) = μ1(A)μ2(B) = ∫ X2μ1(Ey)dμ 2(y).

Hence every rectangle belongs to C. Since the rectangles form a π-system which generates A1 ⊗A2, Theorem 3.8 implies that

C = A1 ⊗A2.

We have therefore proved the theorem for indicator functions of measurable sets. If s = ∑ ⁡ i=1rα i1Ei is a nonnegative simple measurable function, then the partial-integral maps are finite linear combinations of the measurable maps x↦μ2((Ei)x) and y↦μ1((Ei)y). The integral identities follow from the finite additivity and finite linearity already proved for positive integrals. Thus the theorem holds for every nonnegative simple function on X1 × X2. Now let (fn)n≥1 be a sequence of nonnegative simple functions such that fn ↑ f pointwise. For every n, the map

gn(x) := ∫ X2fn(x,y)dμ2(y)

is measurable, and

∫ X1gn(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2fnd(μ1 ⊗ μ2).

For each fixed x in X1, we have fn(x,⋅) ↑ f(x,⋅), so by the monotone convergence theorem

gn(x) ↑ g(x) := ∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y).

Thus g is measurable as the increasing limit of measurable functions, and

∫ X1g(x)dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞∫ X1gn(x)dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞∫ X1×X2fnd(μ1 ⊗ μ2) = ∫ X1×X2fd(μ1 ⊗ μ2).

The same argument with the variables exchanged gives the second iterated formula. This proves the theorem when both measures are finite.

Step 2: the σ-finite case. We now choose increasing sequences (An)n≥1 in A1 and (Bn)n≥1 in A2 such that

X1 = ⋃ n≥1An,X2 = ⋃ n≥1Bn,

and

μ1(An) < ∞,μ2(Bn) < ∞

for every n ≥ 1. For every n ≥ 1, we set

fn := f1An×Bn.

Apply the finite case to the finite measure spaces obtained by restricting X1 to An and X2 to Bn, with the restricted σ-algebras and the restricted measures. The product of the restricted measures agrees, by uniqueness of product measure, with the restriction of μ1 ⊗ μ2 to An × Bn. This gives that

gn(x) := ∫ X2fn(x,y)dμ2(y)

is measurable and that

∫ X1gn(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2fnd(μ1 ⊗ μ2).

For each fixed x in X1, we have fn(x,⋅) ↑ f(x,⋅), hence

gn(x) ↑∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y).

Therefore the map x↦∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y) is measurable, and the monotone convergence theorem yields

∫ X1 (∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y)) dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞∫ X1gn(x)dμ1(x) = lim ⁡ n→∞∫ X1×X2fnd(μ1 ⊗ μ2) = ∫ X1×X2fd(μ1 ⊗ μ2).

The same argument in the other order completes the proof. □

In the particular case of Borel functions, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 10.6 (Tonelli theorem on Borel subsets). Let m,p be two integers ≥ 1, let U ⊂ ℝm and V ⊂ ℝp be Borel sets, and let

f : U × V → [0,∞]

be a Borel function. Then

1.
for every x in U, the section y↦f(x,y) is Borel on V , and the map
x↦∫ V f(x,y)dλp(y)

is Borel on U,

2.
for every y in V , the section x↦f(x,y) is Borel on U, and the map
y↦∫ Uf(x,y)dλm(x)

is Borel on V ,

3.
we have
∫ U×V fdλm+p = ∫ U (∫ V f(x,y)dλp(y)) dλm(x) = ∫ V (∫ Uf(x,y)dλm(x)) dλp(y).

Proof. Step 1. Absolute integrability of almost all sections. We apply Tonelli’s theorem to the spaces U and V endowed with their Borel σ-algebras and with the restrictions of the Lebesgue measures. These spaces are σ-finite, their product σ-algebra is the Borel σ-algebra of U × V , and the corresponding product measure is the restriction of λm+p to U × V . □

Example 10.7 (Area below a graph). Let

f(x,y) := 1{x<y}

on [0, 1]2. For every fixed x in [0, 1], we have

∫ 01f(x,y)dy = ∫ 011 ]x,1](y)dy = 1 − x.

Similarly, for every fixed y in [0, 1],

∫ 01f(x,y)dx = ∫ 011 [0,y[(x)dx = y.

Tonelli’s theorem therefore gives

∫ [0,1]21{x<y}dλ2 = ∫ 01(1 − x)dx = ∫ 01ydy = 1 2.

This is a first concrete computation where the two iterated integrals are both simple and have the same value.

Exercise 10.8 (Area of the unit disk). Let us calculate the area of the unit disk

D = {(x,y) ∈ ℝ2: x2 + y2 ≤ 1}.

By definition, the area of D is the integral of the characteristic function of D over ℝ2; we therefore have

area(D) = ∫ ℝ21D(x,y)dλ2(x,y) = ∫ −11 (∫ −1−x2 1−x2 dy)dx = 2 ∫ −111 − x2dx = 2 ∫ −π∕2π∕2 cos ⁡2(t)dt = π.

The last step uses the fact that the Riemann and Lebesgue integrals coincide for continuous functions on bounded intervals, followed by the classical trigonometric substitution x = sin ⁡(t) in the Riemann sense; this one-dimensional change-of-variables formula will be recalled later in the chapter on change of variables.

10.4 The Fubini theorem for integrable functions

In the case where f is not positive, Tonelli’s theorem first allows us to control the integral of |f|. We may then apply the following statement.

Theorem 10.9 (Fubini theorem). Let (X1,A1,μ1) and (X2,A2,μ2) be two σ-finite measure spaces. Let

f : X1 × X2 → ℝ

be a function in L1(μ 1 ⊗ μ2). Then

1.
there exists a function G in L1(μ 1) such that, for almost every x in X1, the section y↦f(x,y) belongs to L1(μ 2) and
G(x) = ∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y),
2.
there exists a function H in L1(μ 2) such that, for almost every y in X2, the section x↦f(x,y) belongs to L1(μ 1) and
H(y) = ∫ X1f(x,y)dμ1(x),
3.
for any such choices of G and H, we have
∫ X1×X2f(x,y)d(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(x,y) = ∫ X1G(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X2H(y)dμ2(y).

Proof. Step 1. Integrability of almost all sections. We write

f = f+ − f−.

Since f belongs to L1(μ 1 ⊗ μ2), the function |f| = f+ + f− is integrable. Applying Tonelli’s theorem to |f|, we obtain that

g(x) := ∫ X2|f(x,y)|dμ2(y)

defines a positive measurable function on X1 and

∫ X1g(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2|f(x,y)|d(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(x,y) < ∞.

By Lemma 8.8, the set

N := {x ∈ X1: g(x) = +∞}

satisfies μ1(N) = 0. We define the finite representative

g~ := g1X1∖N.

Then g~ is nonnegative and belongs to L1(μ 1).

Step 2. Finite representatives for partial integrals. We now define

g+(x) := ∫ X2f+(x,y)dμ 2(y),g−(x) := ∫ X2f−(x,y)dμ 2(y).

By Tonelli’s theorem, the functions g+ and g− are positive and measurable, and

g+(x) + g−(x) = g(x)

for every x in X1.

We set

G+ := g+1X1∖N,G− := g−1X1∖N,G := G+ − G−.

Then

0 ≤ G+ ≤g~,0 ≤ G− ≤g~,

so G+ and G− belong to L1(μ 1), and therefore G belongs to L1(μ 1). Moreover, for every x in X1 ∖ N, the section y↦f(x,y) belongs to L1(μ 2) and

G(x) = ∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y).

Since G+ = g+ and G− = g− μ1-almost everywhere, Lemma 8.9 and Tonelli’s theorem give

∫ X1G+(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1g+(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2f+d(μ 1 ⊗ μ2), ∫ X1G−(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1g−(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2f−d(μ 1 ⊗ μ2).

Therefore

∫ X1G(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1G+(x)dμ1(x) −∫ X1G−(x)dμ1(x) = ∫ X1×X2f+d(μ 1 ⊗ μ2) −∫ X1×X2f−d(μ 1 ⊗ μ2) = ∫ X1×X2fd(μ1 ⊗ μ2).

This proves item 1 and the first equality in item 3 for the function G constructed above. If G~ is another function satisfying item 1, then G~ = G μ1-almost everywhere, hence

∫ X1G~dμ1 = ∫ X1Gdμ1

by Lemma 8.9.

Step 3. The second variable and the equality of integrals. By symmetry, the same argument applied after exchanging the roles of X1 and X2 proves item 2 and the second equality in item 3. The same uniqueness-up-to-almost-everywhere-equality remark applies to H. □

Remark. Once measurable representatives G and H have been fixed, we usually write item 3 in the shorter form

∫ X1×X2f(x,y)d(μ1 ⊗ μ2)(x,y) = ∫ X1 (∫ X2f(x,y)dμ2(y)) dμ1(x) = ∫ X2 (∫ X1f(x,y)dμ1(x)) dμ2(y).

Only the values of the inner integrals on sets of full measure matter in these formulas.

For integrable Borel functions, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 10.10 (Fubini theorem on Borel subsets). Let m,p be two integers ≥ 1, let U ⊂ ℝm and V ⊂ ℝp be Borel sets, and let

f : U × V → ℝ

be a Borel function integrable with respect to the restriction of λm+p to U × V . Then

1.
there exists a function G in L1(U,λ m) such that, for almost every x in U, the section y↦f(x,y) belongs to L1(V,λ p) and
G(x) = ∫ V f(x,y)dλp(y),
2.
there exists a function H in L1(V,λ p) such that, for almost every y in V , the section x↦f(x,y) belongs to L1(U,λ m) and
H(y) = ∫ Uf(x,y)dλm(x),
3.
for any such choices of G and H, we have
∫ U×V fdλm+p = ∫ UG(x)dλm(x) = ∫ V H(y)dλp(y).

Proof. We apply Fubini’s theorem to the spaces U and V endowed with their Borel σ-algebras and with the restrictions of the Lebesgue measures. These spaces are σ-finite, their product σ-algebra is the Borel σ-algebra of U × V , and the corresponding product measure is the restriction of λm+p to U × V . □

Remark. In the particular case of a continuous function on a bounded closed rectangle U × V of ℝm+p, integrability is automatic, so Fubini’s theorem applies directly. More generally, the relevant hypothesis is integrability, not continuity.

In practice, the theorems of Tonelli and Fubini are used jointly: Tonelli allows us to prove the integrability of a nonnegative function, which is often the key preliminary step before Fubini can be used for an actual computation.

Example 10.11 (A sine integral by Fubini theorem). We have

∫ 0∞e−x sin ⁡x x dx = π 4.

Proof. We consider the function

G(x,y) := e−x cos ⁡(xy)

on ]0,∞[×]0, 1[. For every (x,y) in ]0,∞[×]0, 1[, we have

|G(x,y)|≤ e−x.

Moreover,

∫ ]0,∞[×]0,1[e−xd(λ 1 ⊗ λ1)(x,y) = ∫ 0∞e−xdx = 1,

so the function G is integrable on the product space. Fubini’s theorem therefore applies.

For every x > 0, we have

∫ 01G(x,y)dy = e−x ∫ 01 cos ⁡(xy)dy = e−x sin ⁡x x .

Hence

∫ 0∞e−x sin ⁡x x dx = ∫ 0∞ (∫ 01G(x,y)dy)dx.

Applying Fubini, we obtain

∫ 0∞e−x sin ⁡x x dx = ∫ 01 (∫ 0∞e−x cos ⁡(xy)dx)dy.

It remains to compute the inner integral. For fixed y in ]0, 1[, an antiderivative of x↦e−x cos ⁡(xy) is

x↦e−x ( − cos ⁡(xy) + y sin ⁡(xy) ) 1 + y2 .

Therefore

∫ 0∞e−x cos ⁡(xy)dx = [e−x ( − cos ⁡(xy) + y sin ⁡(xy) ) 1 + y2 ] x=0x=∞ = 1 1 + y2.

We conclude that

∫ 0∞e−x sin ⁡x x dx = ∫ 01 1 1 + y2dy = arctan ⁡(1) − arctan ⁡(0) = π 4.
□
